Sit down at a Magic table today and the first question isn't "what's in your deck?" but "what are we playing?" The game that once had a handful of ways to play now has dozens: competitive and casual, two-player and multiplayer, current and historical, official and invented by players.

Four distinctions sort most of them. In _Constructed_, you build a deck in advance from cards you own. In _Limited_, you build one at the event from cards you open or draft. _Multiplayer_ formats change how many players sit down and how they relate. _Variants_ change the rules, the deckbuilding requirements, or the structure of play. Wizards of the Coast maintains some formats; independent player communities create and govern others.

## Traditional Constructed

These formats descend from conventional 60-card Magic. Most require a deck of at least 60 cards, allow a sideboard of up to 15, and cap each card other than basic lands at four copies. Each keeps its own card pool and banned list.

| Format   | Card pool                                 | Rotation |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------- | -------- |
| Standard | Recent Standard-legal sets                | Yes      |
| Pioneer  | Return to Ravnica (2012) onward           | No       |
| Modern   | Eighth Edition and Mirrodin (2003) onward | No       |
| Legacy   | Nearly all of Magic's history             | No       |
| Vintage  | Nearly all of Magic's history             | No       |
| Pauper   | Cards printed at common                   | No       |

### Standard

Standard is Magic's main rotating format. Its pool covers recent releases, usually about three years' worth, and some sets, Foundations above all, stay legal longer. Each year rotation drops the oldest sets, so the competitive field never settles. Standard is where new designs meet and where the mechanics of the latest sets are tested against one another.

### Pioneer

Pioneer arrived in 2019 and takes qualifying cards from Return to Ravnica (2012) forward. It doesn't rotate. It covers the Theros era and much of contemporary Magic while leaving out the older cards and the Modern Horizons sets that shape Modern, which puts it between Standard's churn and Modern's deeper pool.

### Modern

Modern starts with Eighth Edition and Mirrodin in 2003, plus cards from releases designated Modern-legal, and never rotates. Expect powerful combinations, efficient interaction, and decks honed over years of competition. The Modern Horizons sets, which print strong cards straight into the format, have become some of its biggest influences.

### Legacy

Legacy allows cards from nearly all of Magic's history, minus a long banned list. Old powerhouse spells, odd interactions, and strategies no newer format can support all live here. Legacy is known for efficient spells, intricate stack battles, and archetypes refined over decades.

### Vintage

Vintage is the most permissive traditional competitive format. Instead of banning its most powerful cards, it restricts them to one copy per deck, so the Power Nine (Black Lotus and the original Moxen among them) are legal. The result is extreme efficiency, explosive openings, and interactions drawn from the game's entire history.

### Pauper

Pauper allows only cards with a qualifying printing at common. A card first printed at uncommon or rare becomes legal once it's reprinted at common. The rarity limit doesn't make the format simple: decades of efficient commons have produced strong interactions and durable archetypes. It is also one of the cheaper ways into nonrotating competitive Magic.

## Commander and its relatives

Commander has changed how Magic is played socially. Where traditional Magic is mostly one-on-one, Commander usually seats four.

| Format          | Deck size | Distinguishing feature                                              |
| --------------- | --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Commander (EDH) | 100       | Legendary commander, singleton (one copy of each card), multiplayer |
| Duel Commander  | 100       | Competitive two-player Commander variant                            |
| Oathbreaker     | 60        | Planeswalker and signature spell                                    |

### Commander (EDH)

A Commander deck has 100 cards, one of them the commander, usually a legendary creature, though some cards explicitly allow other types. The commander's color identity limits what the deck can include, and apart from basic lands and named exceptions, every card is a single copy. Players start at 40 life. The commander starts in the command zone and can be cast from there again and again, for a higher cost each time.

Commander rewards other things than duels do: table politics and negotiation, expressive deckbuilding, decks built around a theme or a character, tangled interactions among several players, games that become stories, and social agreements about power and expectations. Commander is the clearest sign of Magic moving toward shared social experience, even though everyone at the table is still trying to win.

#### Commander brackets

Commander uses five brackets to signal what kind of game a deck is built for:

1. Exhibition: strongly themed decks, deliberately low-powered.
2. Core: ordinary social Commander.
3. Upgraded: more focused, more powerful casual decks.
4. Optimized: highly efficient, powerful decks.
5. cEDH: competitive Commander, built to play optimally and win.

These are tiers within one format. The system also names especially strong cards as _Game Changers_, which shape deckbuilding expectations and where a deck lands.

### Duel Commander

Duel Commander adapts Commander for competitive one-on-one play, with its own rules, banned list, and starting life total. Ordinary Commander was built around multiplayer interaction; Duel Commander tunes for the tempo, efficiency, and balance that head-to-head competition needs. An independent community organization maintains it.

### Oathbreaker

Oathbreaker uses a 60-card singleton deck built around two cards: a planeswalker, which is the deck's Oathbreaker, and an instant or sorcery, which is its signature spell. The other 58 cards fill out the deck, all within the planeswalker's color identity. The signature spell can be cast from the command zone while its Oathbreaker is on the battlefield. It's a natural fit for players drawn to the planeswalkers as characters.

## Limited

Limited starts with cards you haven't seen. Instead of bringing a finished deck, you build one at the event from what you open or draft. Most Limited formats require at least 40 cards and allow any number of copies of what you collect.

| Format             | How it works                                               |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Booster Draft      | Typically eight players draft three packs each             |
| Pick-Two Draft     | Four-player draft, selecting two cards per pick            |
| Sealed Deck        | Open six packs and build a deck                            |
| Team Booster Draft | Two teams of three draft and compete                       |
| Team Sealed        | Three players construct three decks from a shared pool     |
| Commander Draft    | Draft 60-card Commander decks                              |
| Conspiracy Draft   | Draft with cards that modify drafting and multiplayer play |
| Cube Draft         | Draft from a curated, reusable collection                  |
| Jumpstart          | Combine two themed 20-card packets                         |

### Booster Draft

A traditional draft seats about eight. Each player opens a pack, takes one card, and passes the rest along; when the packs run out, everyone opens the next one and repeats. Then players build decks from what they took.

Draft tests how well you value cards, spot a strategy as it opens up, read what your neighbors are passing, understand the set's mechanics, and change plans as you learn more. No format shows off a set's design more directly.

### Pick-Two Draft

Pick-Two Draft is built for four players. Each opens three packs and takes two cards every time a pack comes around. It keeps drafting's core decisions in a smaller group.

### Sealed Deck

Each player opens six packs and builds from that pool alone. Nobody competes for individual cards, so the challenge is finding the strongest coherent deck in a pool you didn't choose. Sealed is the usual format at prereleases.

### Team Booster Draft

Two teams of three draft together, then play against members of the other team. Because every pick feeds a team result, the draft gains another layer of strategy.

### Team Sealed

Three teammates share one pool, traditionally twelve packs, and build three decks from it, one each. The format rewards cooperating and dividing cards well among decks that all want the same good ones.

### Commander Draft

Commander Draft joins Limited picking to Commander deckbuilding. Players draft cards, choose commanders, and build 60-card decks, helped by dedicated Commander draft sets whose rules and cards make this practical. It keeps Commander's multiplayer table and makes building the deck a matter of adapting.

### Conspiracy Draft

Conspiracy, first released in 2014, was designed around drafting for multiplayer games. Its signature is cards that act on the draft itself: some change how picks are made, others set up effects or conditions for the games that follow. Conspiracy showed that a draft doesn't have to just hand out cards. The draft can be part of the game.

### Cube Draft

A cube is a player-built Limited environment. Instead of opening fresh boosters, a group drafts from a reusable collection its owner has chosen card by card. The owner decides the cards, the eras represented, the power level, the supported archetypes, the balance among colors, how mechanics interact, and the pace and feel of the games.

One cube might hold only cards from 1997 to 2003. Another might recreate a single block's mechanics, or gather favorite designs from across Magic's history. Cube matters because it shifts real design authority from Wizards to players. A cube is a designed environment that can be tested and refined indefinitely.

### Jumpstart

Jumpstart makes deckbuilding nearly instant. Each player shuffles together two themed 20-card packets into a 40-card deck that's ready to play, and the packets can later be split and recombined. It keeps some of Limited's surprise without a draft or a long build, which makes it good for casual nights and for teaching new players.

## Multiplayer and structural variants

These formats change how a game of Magic is organized.

| Format              | Defining feature                               |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Two-Headed Giant    | Two teams of two players                       |
| Planechase          | Changing global rules represented by planes    |
| Archenemy           | One powerful player against a cooperating team |
| Archenemy Commander | Archenemy played with Commander decks          |
| Freeform            | Highly permissive deck construction            |

### Two-Headed Giant

Two teams of two. Teammates share a life total of 30 and take their turns together, but keep their own decks, hands, and other resources. The format brings cooperation into ordinary Magic: partners decide together how to attack, how to spend resources, and which threats to answer.

### Planechase

Planechase adds a separate deck of oversized plane cards, each a location in Magic's Multiverse, and each plane changes the game's rules. Players roll a special planar die to trigger effects or travel to a new plane. It combines well with other formats, especially Commander.

### Archenemy

Archenemy is lopsided by design. One player becomes the archenemy and faces everyone else, armed with a deck of _scheme_ cards that produce powerful effects. The others team up to bring the archenemy down. It's openly theatrical: one villain, one temporary alliance.

### Archenemy Commander

Archenemy Commander pairs Archenemy's one-against-many structure with Commander deckbuilding, so existing Commander decks can join a game built around a single powerful adversary.

### Freeform

Freeform drops most deckbuilding restrictions. Deck sizes and card counts are whatever the players agree on. It is Magic at its most permissive, defined entirely by the people playing.

## Community-created formats

Some of Magic's most interesting formats began outside Wizards, with their own rules, organizers, and communities. Their survival shows that players can keep a piece of Magic alive, or reinvent it, long after the commercial game has moved on.

| Format                  | Defining feature                                                  |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Premodern               | Fourth Edition through Scourge, with independent bans             |
| Old School (93/94)      | Magic's earliest releases                                         |
| Middle School           | Historical environment centered on the late 1990s and early 2000s |
| Canadian Highlander     | 100-card singleton with a points system                           |
| Pauper Commander        | Commander using commons and an uncommon creature commander        |
| Peasant                 | Commons with a limited allowance for uncommons                    |
| Artisan                 | Commons and uncommons                                             |
| Tribal Wars             | Decks built around a particular creature type                     |
| Tiny Leaders            | 50-card singleton decks with inexpensive commanders               |
| Battle Box              | Shared curated cards for immediate play                           |
| Dandan / Forgetful Fish | Specialized shared-deck Magic                                     |

### Premodern

Premodern covers Fourth Edition (1995) through Scourge (2003): the core sets from Fourth through Seventh Edition, Ice Age and Alliances, and the Mirage, Tempest, Urza, Mercadian Masques, Invasion, Odyssey, and Onslaught blocks. That span includes the Exodus, Urza, Classic Sixth Edition, and Masques years.

It plays under today's rules and keeps its own banned list to keep games interesting and stop any one strategy from taking over. Premodern is a strong example of players stewarding a format themselves. Its community keeps that era running as a living competitive game.

### Old School (93/94)

Old School tries to recreate Magic as it first was. Its pool centers on Alpha, Beta, Unlimited, Arabian Nights, Antiquities, Legends, and The Dark, with exact legality set by whichever ruleset a community follows. Several traditions exist, Swedish and Atlantic among them, and they differ on reprints, permitted cards, and banned or restricted lists. Old School tends to prize original cards, period aesthetics, and the social culture of the game's first years.

### Middle School

Middle School fills the historical gap between Old School and the later nonrotating formats. Its card pool and rules depend on the community running it, but it usually centers on the late 1990s and early 2000s, aiming to keep that period's mechanics and strategies in play. Don't confuse it with Premodern, whose card pool is more clearly defined and more established.

### Canadian Highlander

Canadian Highlander uses 100-card singleton decks with no commander, and players start at 20 life. Its signature is a points list. Instead of banning every powerful card, the format assigns points to the strongest ones and caps the total a deck may hold. You can play the best cards, but each one costs you something else. Power itself becomes a scarce resource to spend.

### Pauper Commander

Pauper Commander joins Commander's multiplayer singleton structure to a rarity limit. The commander is generally an uncommon creature, and the other 99 cards need qualifying printings at common. Its rules and banned list are maintained separately from Commander's. The format rewards inventive decks built from cheap, often-overlooked cards.

### Peasant

Peasant is a rarity-limited Constructed format: commons plus a small allowance of uncommons. The allowance varies by ruleset, though five is a familiar number. In strategy, it sits between Pauper and unrestricted Constructed.

### Artisan

Artisan allows commons and uncommons and excludes rares and mythics. Next to Pauper, it opens up stronger build-around cards and a wider range of strategies. It has no single standardized ruleset.

### Tribal Wars

Tribal Wars builds decks around one creature type: Goblins, Elves, Zombies, Soldiers, Merfolk, and so on. Construction rules vary by organization. The format celebrates creature identity and the mechanics that have grown up around particular tribes over Magic's history.

### Tiny Leaders

Tiny Leaders uses 50-card singleton decks led by a commander whose mana value (its total mana cost) is three or less, and the rest of the deck is held to similarly cheap cards. It was designed as a faster two-player alternative to Commander. Its popularity has fallen well below its early peak.

### Battle Box

Battle Box skips deckbuilding. Players draw from a shared, curated collection, often with special arrangements for mana, so they can start a meaningful game with almost no setup. Designs vary widely.

### Dandan / Forgetful Fish

Dandan, named for the card Dandân, is a shared-deck format. Both players draw from one library and often share a graveyard. The deck is built from a small set of cards and some unusual library-manipulation effects, and because both players draw from the same pile, every choice changes what the opponent can do. Dandan shows how a handful of existing cards can be rearranged into something very unlike ordinary Magic.

## Historical formats and names

Magic's competitive structure has been rebuilt more than once.

| Historical format | Later development                         |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Type 1            | Became Vintage                            |
| Type 1.5          | Evolved into Legacy                       |
| Type 2            | Became Standard                           |
| Extended          | Retired in 2013                           |
| Block Constructed | Disappeared from regular competitive play |
| Frontier          | Largely superseded by Pioneer             |

### Extended

Extended had a larger pool than Standard but periodically limited how far back players could reach. It was meant as a competitive middle ground between Standard and the broadest historical formats, and Modern eventually took over much of that ground.

### Block Constructed

Block Constructed limited players to a single block of expansions. Tempest Block Constructed, for example, allowed Tempest, Stronghold, and Exodus. It let players study how one set of mechanics worked as a mostly self-contained system. It no longer has a regular place in organized play, but the idea survives in custom Constructed environments and in Cube.

### Frontier

Frontier was a community-built nonrotating format starting with Magic 2015. It drew real attention before Pioneer arrived; then Pioneer took over much the same territory and became the more widely adopted format.

## Choosing where to return

For someone who started in the middle of the Urza block, with cards on the table reaching back to Exodus, played through Classic Sixth Edition and Mercadian Masques, came back during Theros, and is returning now, four formats stand out.

### Premodern: recover the game you knew

Premodern covers that first era directly. It's a way to meet familiar cards and mechanics again in a maintained competitive environment, and to see how decades of rules changes have altered the way those original cards play.

### Cube: design the game you want

Cube is the furthest from the cycle of new releases. You choose the card pool, the eras, the mechanical themes, and the style of play. A careful cube can recreate a specific period or build a new blend of Magic's past, and it rewards understanding the game as a designer as well as a player.

### Pioneer: meet contemporary competitive Magic

Pioneer is a good entry into today's nonrotating Constructed. Its starting line falls just before Theros, which ties it to your second stretch of play. It keeps classic two-player competition while taking in a large share of modern card design.

### Commander: understand Magic's social side

Commander is essential to understanding how Magic's culture has changed. Multiplayer tables, singleton decks, legendary characters, and informal social agreements add up to a very different idea of play from 60-card competition. Even players who end up preferring duels gain from seeing how much Commander shapes the game now.
