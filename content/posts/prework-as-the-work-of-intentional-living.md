---
title: Prework as the work of intentional living
description: Argues that preparatory tasks—laying out gym clothes, blocking calendars, writing tomorrow's priorities—are where values turn into acts, drawing on Seneca and Socrates, and notes the claim fails when preparation never ends in action.
date: 2026-11-30
---

Prework usually means the tasks done before the main event: reviewing an agenda before a meeting, prepping ingredients before cooking, stretching before a run. These feel instrumental, even menial, subordinate to the "real" work. That ranking is wrong. For anyone living intentionally, the preparation is the practice itself.

Living deliberately means anticipating: projecting yourself into tomorrow and arranging the conditions for the person you intend to be. Laying out the gym clothes, blocking the calendar, writing tomorrow's priorities tonight—this is where values turn into acts. Deciding to exercise is easy; setting up the morning so you will is where the commitment lives.

People routinely confuse who they mean to be with who they are, and prework fills that gap or leaves it open. Seneca's complaint that people squander life through inattention covers skipping this work. The examined life Socrates demanded looks forward as well as back.

Intentional living sounds lofty, but it comes down to labor, mostly unglamorous and unseen. Holding the right values doesn't make a life; the tedious work that puts them to use does.

Prework fails at one extreme: the person forever organizing and never acting. It has to end in the work. The shoes by the door are the decision.
