---
title: Building a local lunar calendar for Rockford, Michigan
description: Proposes a naturalistic pagan practice rooted in the Rogue River watershed, beginning lunar months at the visible crescent rather than astronomical conjunction, and building phenological moon names from years of local observation instead of borrowed Wheel of the Year or Indigenous terminology.
date: 2026-11-12
---

A radically local pagan practice in Rockford, Michigan, would begin with an odd reversal. Modern people have clocks more accurate than anything imagined by the agricultural societies that organized their months by the Moon, yet most of us know less about the passage of time in the places where we live. We know when March begins but not when the ground thaws. We know the date of the autumnal equinox but not the week when the first hard frost usually reaches our neighborhood. We can get the exact instant of astronomical conjunction from a phone while remaining unaware that the first spring peepers began calling two nights earlier in the marsh down the road. The old lunar calendars were less precise in one sense and more intimate in another. They placed people inside a sequence of changes that had to be watched because livelihood depended on recognizing them. A naturalistic or atheopagan practice in Rockford could not recover that necessity, nor should it pretend to, but it could recover the habit of attention from which such calendars arose.

The Moon suits this purpose: its cycle falls between the day and the year. Its synodic period of about twenty-nine and a half days is long enough for the landscape to change visibly and short enough that a person can remember what it looked like one cycle earlier. Before printed calendars, the first returning crescent after the dark of the Moon was a public event; the Romans called the first day of the month the Kalends, from _calare_, "to call out." In agricultural societies the Moon became entangled with planting, harvesting, debt and sacrifice.

Some of the causal theories attached to those practices were wrong. Crops do not prosper because they were planted while the Moon was waxing, and timber does not acquire mysterious virtues merely because it was felled while the Moon was waning. A 2020 review in the journal _Agronomy_ found no reliable evidence supporting agricultural scheduling by lunar phase, and while a 2023 study in _Plants_ did detect physiological responses in plants exposed to full moonlight, that is a far narrower proposition than the traditional claim that sowing under a particular phase improves a crop. Strip away the magic and another function shows. The practices organized recurrent attention.

The Moon need not exert an occult influence on beans, cattle or human intentions for its phases to frame how people notice beginnings, growth, culmination, decline and rest; the lit face of a waxing Moon does grow, and the dark Moon does disappear. Atheopaganism, the naturalistic path Mark Green founded, treats ritual as a human technology rather than a way of manipulating supernatural forces, and so changes the question from what power the Moon exerts over us to what kind of relationship with time we create by repeatedly attending to it.

Much modern lunar practice has a problem. The astronomical new moon is conjunction, when the Moon is effectively invisible. Modern calendars can identify that instant to the minute, so many people now perform "new moon" observances without ever seeing the Moon. Older systems often treated the first visible crescent as the meaningful beginning instead. That difference is more than antiquarian. It separates a calculated event from an encountered one. For a practice meant to restore attention to place, the crescent is the better start. Green proposes exactly this in "The Atheopagan Calendar of Moons" (2020), beginning each lunar cycle on the evening the thin crescent first appears. We would not begin the month because an application announced that conjunction occurred at 3:17 in the afternoon. We would go outside after sunset during the following evenings and look west. Sometimes cloud would obscure the horizon. Sometimes trees would interfere. Sometimes the crescent would be found immediately, sometimes not until the next evening, and sometimes not at all. Cloud is common here and everywhere else, so not seeing the Moon is part of the story. A clouded evening is weather, and weather is what the practice watches; the entry for that night is the overcast itself. Nor is the evening crescent the only way to meet the Moon. In the last mornings of a cycle the old crescent rises thin in the east before dawn, and its disappearance opens the dark days. Near the full, moonlight brightens the cloud from behind even when the face never shows. The uncertainty would be an advantage. It would return the beginning of the month to the weather and the sky.

The dark days before the first crescent can do other work. Ancient Greek household religion distinguished the ending of the lunar month from its renewal. Hekate's Deipnon belonged to the closing darkness and involved purifying the house, atoning for offenses and leaving the leftovers at a crossroads; Noumenia marked the first visible crescent and the opening of the next month. There is no reason for a naturalistic practice in Michigan to reproduce those rites, but the structure meets ordinary human needs. A month can end before another begins. Clean the tools, finish what can be finished, check the stores, review the accounts, let some plans go. Then the crescent appears and the work turns from finishing to starting. The sequence works without believing that Hekate presides over the crossroads or that the Moon transmits a force of renewal.

The harder part is making the practice local. Modern paganism has inherited a large body of seasonal material from northern and western Europe, especially the eight-festival Wheel of the Year. That calendar can be meaningful as a modern religious construction, but not when its pastoral imagery is made to fit everywhere. February in western Michigan is not early spring in any ordinary ecological sense. John Halstead, writing from the southern tip of Lake Michigan, made this criticism directly in "Why the Wheel of the Year doesn't make sense to me anymore" (2024), describing lake-effect weather, "three or so" winters, and a friend who proposed marking the seasons by the sound of acorns falling on her roof.

Jon Cleland Host, a Michigan naturalistic pagan who launched a website for the movement in 2004 and now edits naturalisticpaganism.org, has carried the same objection past criticism. Imbolc is a winter festival in Michigan, whatever it was in the British Isles—and rather than leave it at that, he renamed the cross-quarters outright, coining _thermistice_ and _equitherm_ in place of the inherited pastoral vocabulary. The movement's main site now uses them in its own guide to ritual. That is the best evidence so far that a locally derived calendar can be more than a private exercise: new terms, proposed by one person, taken up by others.

A May Day festival inherited from Britain may coincide with leafing trees and flowering plants in Rockford, but only by chance. Popular lists of moon names, assembled from Indigenous, European and modern almanac traditions, have the same problem: a "Wolf Moon," "Strawberry Moon" or "Harvest Moon" becomes a decorative label detached from the people and ecological circumstances that produced it.

Rockford already has a place from which a calendar could grow. The most useful boundary is not the city limit but the Rogue River watershed and the surrounding agricultural and wooded country of northern Kent County. The Rogue drains roughly 234 square miles of Kent and Newaygo counties before joining the Grand River. The river gives the area a physical coherence that municipal geography cannot: water from fields, wetlands, subdivisions, woodlots and tributaries all enters the same system. The watershed includes working agriculture, cold-water streams, wooded corridors and developed land. Its condition records what happens across the landscape: snowmelt, heavy rain, drought, erosion, runoff, restoration, seasonal vegetation and human use. A person returning to the same stretch of river every lunar cycle would encounter a place that is recognizably constant and continuously different.

That repeated encounter suggests the proper relationship between solar and lunar time. Agriculture cannot be lunar because the seasons are solar. Twelve lunar cycles amount to only about 354 days, so a purely lunar calendar slips through the seasonal year. Ancient agrarian societies that depended on lunar months had to solve this problem by adding extra months or otherwise anchoring their calendars to the Sun. The reason was practical. A month called "harvest" becomes useless if it gradually migrates into winter. Rockford's local calendar would therefore need two clocks. The solar year would set the broad conditions of life: changing day length, accumulated heat and cold, dormancy, growth and dying back. Weather would determine the particular character of each year. The lunar cycle would provide the repeated interval at which the landscape is examined again.

Maple season shows the distinction. Sap does not begin to run because a particular lunar month has arrived. It responds primarily to the alternation of freezing nights and warmer days near the end of winter. Michigan State University Extension says as much: producers in southern Michigan usually start tapping in late February, those in the Upper Peninsula weeks later, and most seasons arrive as a series of short sap runs rather than one steady flow. In one year those conditions may arrive early; in another they may be delayed. A preassigned "Sap Moon" therefore puts the calendar ahead of the phenomenon. A better practice would reverse the order. The crescent appears, the observer walks outside, and the question is whether the conditions of sap season have begun. Are nights still falling below freezing? Are daytime temperatures rising? Is snow retreating from open ground while remaining in shaded woods? Is the Rogue carrying meltwater? Are maple buds changing? If sap is running, the name belongs to that cycle. If winter holds, it does not.

The same approach works all year. We would begin not with twelve names but with several years of observation. Anna Walther's "Creating a Local Lunar Calendar: Full Moon Names for Central Texas" (2015) is the nearest precedent for building the vocabulary from where we live, and her "Home Base Practice" (2015)—which credits Starhawk for the underlying idea—names the habit of returning to one chosen spot often enough that seasonal change becomes visible. At each first crescent, return to the same places and record a small number of things: the condition of the air, water, vegetation, animals and human work. The record need not be elaborate. Elaboration might defeat the purpose by turning a durable practice into a short-lived project. A few sentences maintained for twenty years would be more valuable than a sophisticated database abandoned after one. The March entry might note high brown water, snow remaining in ravines, maple sap running and geese overhead. A later entry might record peepers calling, buds breaking and tractors appearing in fields. By early summer there would be cottonwood seed, mosquitoes, thunderstorms, emerging corn and long evening light. September would bring cooling nights, ripening apples, drying field corn, migrating birds and the first isolated color in maples. November might be bare branches, saturated ground, emptied fields and the first persistent ice along quiet water.

After several years, names will suggest themselves. Sap may prove reliable enough to deserve one. Frog-Calling would be more precise than a generic Spring Moon. Leaf-Out, Blossom, Hay, Long-Light, Apple, Leaf-Fall, Frozen-Ground or River-Ice may emerge. Some will fail. A supposedly dependable event will wander too widely from year to year, while another unnoticed phenomenon proves consistent. The calendar would become valuable because it had not been designed in advance. Its vocabulary would be a conclusion drawn from acquaintance.

Such a system would resemble older ecological calendars in method without pretending to inherit them. Māori maramataka, for example, organized individual lunar nights, as well as months, in relation to fishing, planting, harvesting and other activities, with important local variation among communities. Michigan should take the method and leave the names: a mature calendar arises from repeated observation of relationships among celestial cycles, weather, species and work. The same caution applies to Anishinaabe traditions of the Great Lakes. They have their own histories and cultural contexts. A new naturalistic practice around Rockford would gain nothing by borrowing Indigenous terminology to appear ancient. Its integrity would depend on accepting that it is new and allowing age to accumulate honestly.

That acceptance is the project's most radical element. Modern spiritual movements often want complete systems immediately: named festivals, established correspondences, prescribed rituals and an origin story. A local tradition would begin in ignorance. During its first years it would know very little. After a decade it might know when certain birds usually return or when particular trees normally leaf out. After thirty years it would contain memories of winters and droughts younger participants had never experienced. After seventy years the notebook itself would have become inherited knowledge. There is no need to manufacture antiquity when tradition can be understood as sustained memory.

The practice stays agricultural even for people who do not farm. Rockford sits in a region where corn, soybeans, hay, fruit, maple production, gardens and orchards continue to shape the surrounding landscape. The scale is not nostalgic: Michigan's 2025 apple crop was forecast at about 1.26 billion pounds, and the Fruit Ridge orchards lie immediately west of Rockford. Modern food distribution allows residents to ignore most of these rhythms, which makes deliberate attention matter more. A person could learn when local sweet corn appears rather than when supermarkets first stock corn from elsewhere, when hay is being cut, and when apples move through their varieties. No one has to play preindustrial farmer. The point is to know what the countryside is doing.

The lunar phases could give these activities a rhythm without pretending to govern them. Waxing can be for beginning and tending: planting when soil and weather permit, maintaining gardens, starting repairs, undertaking projects or learning something new about the watershed. Full moon nights are naturally suited to gathering because they alter the nocturnal landscape; snow under a January full moon and an open field under a June one are different from dark nights.

Waning could become a conventional period for completion, preservation, pruning in season, removing invasive plants, giving things away or finishing neglected work. The dark moon could close the cycle with rest, accounting and disposal. It is also the time to look at what has been stored. A jar of September apples opened in January is summer carried through winter, and no one has to impose a symbol on it. These associations would be human conventions rather than laws of nature, but conventions help so long as reality outranks them. Tomatoes should be harvested because they are ripe, not because the Moon is waning.

Annual observances could grow the same way. Astronomical events such as solstices and equinoxes are fixed because they describe real positions in Earth's orbit, but ecological thresholds must be free to move. First substantial thaw, first frog call, river opening, first blossom, first fireflies, first ripe apple, first hard frost, first lasting snow: these are events rather than dates. Some are easy to read here. Wild lupine comes into bloom in the oak-pine barrens of the Maas Family Nature Preserve in late spring, and the endangered Karner blue butterfly depends on it—a date no one can schedule, tied to a species anyone can check. A Feast of First Frost could not be printed permanently on a calendar. People would have to wait for frost. Once it occurred, the following crescent might open the frost-season month and provide an occasion for a meal from late harvest and stored food. In another year the frost might arrive two weeks earlier or later. The variation would be part of what the tradition knows.

Over time the notebook would also track the climate. Conventional calendars conceal environmental change because October 15 remains October 15 forever. Phenology—the timing of natural events—shows the shift. If sap begins earlier, snow cover becomes less persistent, migratory arrivals move, summer smoke becomes recurrent, or first frost moves later, the accumulated observations will show it. A practice begun as religion or philosophy could therefore become a modest form of citizen science. It would also create a way of remembering environmental change in ordinary language: the winter the Rogue never really froze, the April freeze that ruined the blossoms, the summer of smoke, the year the old maple came down, the season when drought lowered the river unusually early.

That is how local mythology can arise without supernatural invention. At its most basic, myth is a community's remembered account of its place and its relationship to forces larger than individual lives. A flood, a vanished orchard, a restored stream: each becomes a story told years later. Children learn where the river used to overflow, which tree stood before the storm, which winter produced snow taller than they have ever seen. Repetition gives certain events weight; memory connects generations. Eventually people live in the landscape's history as well as its ground.

This is where a local paganism diverges from generalized nature spirituality. "Nature" is an abstraction capacious enough to mean almost anything. Place is restrictive. Rockford means these soils, this river, these winters, these farms, these roads, these woodlots and this particular history of settlement and alteration. It includes subdivisions as well as wetlands, invasive plants as well as admired native ones, storm drains as well as trout streams. A river passing through a town can be admired while remaining poorly understood; treat the Rogue as sacred, and its tributaries, pollutants, erosion and cold-water habitat become matters worth knowing. The Kent Conservation District is a partner in a federal project, led by the Lower Grand River Organization of Watersheds, to fund farm practices such as buffer strips that cut sedimentation in the Rogue watershed. If soil is sacred, the condition of agricultural soil matters. If trees are sacred, learning to identify several species in winter should follow. If migration matters, we should know which birds leave and return. Naturalism need not mean disenchantment; it may instead remove the habit of looking through the world toward something supposedly behind it. And if there is no hidden spiritual realm that gives the Rogue its significance, the Rogue itself has to bear the weight. In a naturalistic sense, sacredness measures how seriously people keep faith with a place and its creatures.

This piece appears on Thursday, November 12. The Moon was new early Monday morning, and tonight it is a crescent three days old, low in the southwest after sunset and setting a few hours later. Go out and look for it. Others will be looking too, in their own yards and fields, at the same thin light. If cloud covers it, look at the cloud. Then write down what the evening holds—the air, the water, whatever is still green, whatever has gone—and keep the page until the crescent returns in December.

The crescent provides neither an explanation nor a command. It sets an appointment: twenty-nine or thirty days have passed, so we go back and look again, knowing the landscape will not be what it was. The practice itself need not be elaborate. During the dark moon, we close what can be closed. When the crescent shows, we go outside and find it if the weather lets us, or note the sky if it does not, then walk ground we know—the Rogue, or another place chosen and kept—and record enough to compare when the crescent comes back. Kept long enough, the record stops being a log. Names stop being inventions and become descriptions; festivals answer events instead of dates; memories gather around years the ordinary calendar treats as interchangeable. Agricultural societies watched the Moon because they needed a calendar. Modern Rockford does not; the old necessity can become a chosen discipline. The phone can tell us to the minute when another month has begun. It cannot tell us what happened here while it passed.
