---
title: The year turns slowly, not at midnight
description: Revisits the author's own claim that the year fades from late autumn and turns only in spring, keeping solstices, equinoxes, and lunar phases while refusing the neo-pagan wheel of the year. Concedes the claim is northern and temperate and that January 1 is a social clock in its own right, and finds its best point in the winter, when the returning light and the deepening cold belong to different years.
date: 2026-10-01
---

The year does not turn at midnight on December 31. It dies slowly across five months, from late autumn into spring, and the new one arrives only when the light has visibly returned. I once put it in three sentences: "The old year is not replaced by the new year in one grand celebration on January 1. The old year starts to fade away in late autumn, and the new year doesn't replace it until spring. Equinox and Solstice, new moon and full, not a neo-Celtic wheel, but still time and cycle."

The claim is about lived time, not astronomy or the civil calendar. January 1 is a convention, adopted by the Romans as the day consuls took office, and it has no natural correlate in the northern hemisphere's sky or soil. Nothing happens on that date: the solstice has already passed, the coldest weeks lie ahead, and the days are only just lengthening. A person who feels the year turning at New Year's is feeling a social fact. I meant that the felt year ends with the decline of light and growth through October and November, and begins with the thaw and greening of March and April. The civil boundary describes the year poorly.

Older practice backs the claim. Many agrarian calendars placed their year-turn at points tied to the agricultural cycle rather than to midwinter: the Roman year itself began in March until the second century BC, which is why September through December still carry the names of the seventh through tenth months. The English legal year began on March 25 until 1752. Even the academic year and many countries' fiscal years turn elsewhere. I was pointing at something historically normal: many year-boundaries are the rule, and a single January date is the anomaly.

The disclaimer in my last sentence matters. The eight-spoke wheel of the year, with its Samhain, Yule, Imbolc, Ostara, and the rest, is largely a twentieth-century construction, assembled out of scattered folklore by Gerald Gardner's Wicca and Ross Nichols's Druidry, and its claim to Celtic antiquity is weak. In refusing it, I avoided trading one imposed schedule for another. What I kept is more modest and more defensible: the four astronomical quarter points, which are observable and independent of any tradition, and the lunar phases, which are among the oldest timekeeping humans have. With "still time and cycle" I meant that the underlying structure holds even when the neo-pagan packaging does not.

But I assumed a temperate northern-hemisphere climate with a strong seasonal signal; in the tropics, where the main variable is rainfall, or in the southern hemisphere, where autumn arrives in March, the specific dates fail, though the general principle of a gradual transition survives. I also assumed that the natural cycle should govern the felt year, whereas much of modern life is organized around budgets, semesters, and fiscal quarters that make January 1 a hinge for the people living under them. I was right that the civil boundary is arbitrary, but arbitrary is not the same as inert: a convention that coordinates millions of people's plans, resolutions, and accounts does produce a transition, a social one. My lines overreach in implying the civil celebration is false, when it is a second, overlaid clock.

My middle claim is loose. Read literally, it gives the winter to the old year: the year fades from late autumn and is not replaced until spring, so December through February belong to a dying year. The solstice splits that ownership. After it the light belongs to the new year, lengthening a little each day, while the cold still belongs to the old, deepening into January. For two months the sky and the ground keep different calendars, and the winter belongs fully to neither. That interval interests me most. It is a year with a gap in it, a dormant stretch with no single owner. Whether that is a flaw in the account or its deepest point depends on whether time must always be someone's year. At its best, the claim describes a season the calendar has no name for. What would a year look like if its dead center were a pause and not a seam?
