---
title: Humans have continuity of life, agents have continuity of task
description: "You are meat in the physical world: hungry, tired, called away. The agent you work with is none of those things. What does it owe a collaborator whose attention keeps leaving?"
date: 2027-01-06
---

When you work with an AI agent, remember: you are meat in the physical world, with many distractions. It is not, and will not be.

This is the asymmetry in human–AI collaboration: the human has continuity of life; the agent has continuity of task.

You live in a physical world that makes competing demands on your attention. You get hungry, tired, interrupted, distracted, and called away. You change places. You remember something unrelated. Your priorities shift while you're doing something else.

The agent experiences none of this. It has no biological needs, no household, no passing traffic, no unexpected visitor, and no competing sensory environment. Within its context, the task is its whole world.

But an agent is not always attentive. It can be suspended, lose context, run out of resources, or resume without knowing what happened while you were gone. What it lacks is the human experience of interruption.

Your silence tells the agent nothing. It cannot tell whether you are thinking, distracted, asleep, dissatisfied, or simply making coffee. Do not expect it to read your silence as agreement, abandonment, or permission.

You should not have to maintain the agent's continuity. Expect a well-designed agent to preserve decisions, outstanding questions, assumptions, and progress, so you can pick the work back up without reconstructing the entire conversation.

Your attention is the scarce resource. The agent's ability to produce another thousand words is not evidence that those words deserve a thousand words' worth of your attention. Expect it to make its work cheap to understand, evaluate, and direct.

Nor should you have to keep pace with the agent. You can interrupt, change direction, return hours later, or abandon a line of inquiry without ceremony. The agent should absorb your gaps.

Tools have always demanded your sustained attention. The bargain was simple: the tool supplied force or speed, and you supplied continuity, keeping the work's shape in your head from one moment to the next. Put the tool down and the work stopped where you left it, held only in memory. An agent reverses the bargain. It can hold the structure of the work while your attention moves elsewhere.

The ideal is not a human who learns to think and work like an agent. It is an agent designed around the reality that humans do not.

The human owns the purpose. The agent maintains the thread.
