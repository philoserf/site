---
title: How companies drift into failure
description: Explores the aphorism that companies fail gradually through accumulated comfortable half-truths rather than single dramatic mistakes, using Kodak, Nokia, and Blockbuster as examples, and distinguishes this drift from failures caused by external constraints or shocks.
date: 2026-12-09
---

No company decides to fail, the saying goes; it drifts there one comfortable lie at a time. The aphorism compresses a theory of organizational decline into two short clauses that shift attention away from dramatic mistakes and toward the ordinary habits that make those mistakes possible. We usually tell business failure as the story of a single disastrous acquisition, a technological disruption, or a financial crisis. It argues instead that collapse is usually the visible end of a long process in which people repeatedly choose reassuring interpretations over accurate ones until reality stops being negotiable.

The key word is not "lie" but "comfortable." Organizations run on optimistic forecasts, softened reports, selective metrics, and strategic narratives that sit somewhere between deception and sincere belief. Few executives begin the day intending to mislead investors, employees, or themselves. More often, incentives reward confidence, punish unwelcome news, and encourage every layer of management to present structural weaknesses as temporary deviations. Someone nudges a sales forecast up because the previous quarter recovered at the last minute. Managers attribute dissatisfaction to unusual circumstances. They frame rising costs as investments that will soon pay off. Each judgment appears defensible. The cumulative effect is a widening gap between the organization's internal picture of itself and the conditions under which it actually operates.

Kodak did not fail because it lacked knowledge of digital photography; its own engineers pioneered much of it. Nokia's engineers could build smartphones; the company could not bring itself to bet on them. People inside Blockbuster imagined streaming; the company ignored it anyway. In each case, the information already existed inside the organization. The hard part was accepting what the information implied about existing business models, power structures, and assumptions. Comfortable interpretations delayed painful decisions until competitors had established decisive advantages. The drift is epistemic before it is financial. A company fails because it gradually loses the ability to perceive itself honestly.

Drift implies no one is navigating; it does not imply anyone is aiming for the rocks. Most organizations retain the appearance of purposeful movement long after strategic control has weakened. Managers meet quarterly targets through accounting adjustments, discounts, acquisitions, or deferred maintenance. Customer complaints rise slowly enough that each month's increase appears manageable. Experienced employees leave one by one. Standards slip one exception at a time. Every step seems reversible until the accumulated distance from reality becomes too great. By the time the crisis is visible from outside, the habits that produced it are embedded.

Even so, the aphorism risks making organizational decline appear more psychologically unified than it is. Companies do not always fail because they believe pleasant falsehoods. Some fail while seeing the truth clearly. A manufacturer may understand perfectly that overseas competitors enjoy structural cost advantages but lack the capital to modernize. A newspaper may accurately predict the consequences of digital advertising while remaining trapped by debts incurred decades earlier. External shocks—a pandemic, war, regulatory change, technological discontinuity—sometimes overwhelm competent organizations whose leaders are under no comforting illusions. In those cases, constraints produce the failure.

The word "lie" also carries more moral weight than most organizational failures earn. Many damaging beliefs are not lies in the ordinary sense because nobody consciously fabricates them. Human beings are adept at motivated reasoning. Executives convince themselves that warning signs are temporary because admitting otherwise would require layoffs, abandoning cherished strategies, or acknowledging years of mistaken judgment. Middle managers filter bad news because they have learned that promotion favors optimism. Employees remain silent because previous criticism was ignored or punished. The resulting picture is false, but its falsity emerges from ordinary cognitive and institutional pressures rather than coordinated dishonesty. Replacing "lie" with "illusion" would capture more of the psychology while sacrificing some rhetorical force.

The strongest reading treats the aphorism as a description of organizational epistemology rather than ethics. It is less concerned with whether people are truthful than with whether institutions can keep correcting themselves as reality changes. Healthy companies build mechanisms that make comfortable stories difficult to sustain: dissenting voices with real authority, incentives aligned with long-term outcomes, measurements no one can easily game, cultures in which unwelcome information travels upward quickly rather than disappearing in successive layers of approval. Their resilience comes from systems that expose mistakes before they become irreversible, not from leaders who never make them.

Read that way, the aphorism reaches beyond business. Families, governments, universities, and nonprofits all depend on their capacity to replace reassuring narratives with accurate ones before circumstances force the issue. The danger is not occasional optimism; it is the slow trade of external correspondence for internal coherence. Institutions remain viable only while they permit reality to revise their self-understanding faster than habit can defend it. Failure begins when preserving confidence matters more than preserving contact with the world. The balance sheet turns red much later.
