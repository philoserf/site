---
title: "Magic: The Gathering endures as a game despite commercial excess"
description: Magic made $1.7 billion in 2025, and its newest Commander decks cost $74.99. The game is thriving. How much of what made it worth playing can survive a company this determined to sell?
date: 2027-04-05
---

Magic: The Gathering, which Richard Garfield created and Wizards of the Coast published in 1993, is still one of the most sophisticated competitive games ever made, a rich medium for personal expression, and an increasingly aggressive business. Garfield built it partly to meet the need for a game that was quick to play with minimal equipment and would go over well at conventions, and his main mechanical inspiration was Cosmic Encounter, whose wildly different alien powers showed how exceptions to shared rules could generate variety.

Mark Rosewater, Magic's longtime head designer, names Garfield's three founding ideas: the trading card game itself, the color wheel, and the mana system. The first let players build their own playing pieces before sitting down to compete, so paper Magic is two activities wound together: designing a deck from a huge catalog of possibilities, then finding out what that design can do against another person. Together the three still work well.

In person, the game grows another layer. Reading an opponent's hesitation, tracking which lands are still untapped, counting cards in hand, deciding whether to reveal a valuable spell: none of it is in the printed rules. Players keep the game state by hand, spot interactions, and settle disagreements. That invites mistakes, muddled board states, and tiring complexity, but it also demands shared understanding. A game of paper Magic is a negotiation over what is happening as well as a contest over what should happen next.

Commander has become Magic's dominant social format. It started as a community-made variant and is usually played by four people with hundred-card singleton decks, each led by a legendary creature as its commander. Its appeal is creative self-expression and multiplayer politics. Alliances form, promises get made, and a player with a weak board can still sway stronger opponents. Its weaknesses are just as real: long turns, mismatched decks, and the chance that one player's elaborate combo leaves the other three watching instead of playing.

Commander also shows how hard it is to govern a community-run game whose cards are worth money. In September 2024 the independent Rules Committee banned Mana Crypt, Jeweled Lotus, Dockside Extortionist, and Nadu, Winged Wisdom. The decision brought severe harassment and threats, and management of the format passed to Wizards. The company's five-bracket system that followed tries to separate casual play from optimized and competitive decks. A February 2026 update named wordy cards, redundant effects, and "self-solving" commanders as design trends the panel had pushed back on. Those are telling admissions: the most creative format risks being standardized by the company that makes its pieces.

Commercially, Magic is thriving. Hasbro reported $1.7 billion in Magic revenue for 2025 and more than one million unique organized-play participants. The third quarter of 2026 was Magic's first $600 million quarter, and by then more than 13,000 stores belonged to the Wizards Play Network. These are the company's own figures, but they put to rest claims that tabletop Magic is dying. The paper community is still large, held up by neighborhood game stores, Friday Night Magic, Regional Championships, professional tournaments, and big MagicCon gatherings.

The bigger change is Magic's turn into a platform for other entertainment franchises. Universes Beyond has brought Final Fantasy, Marvel, The Hobbit, and Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles into the game. Four of 2026's seven major releases were built on outside properties, the last of them Star Trek in November. The strategy reaches people who might never have met traditional Magic. It also strains a fictional universe once held together by places like Dominaria, Ravnica, and Innistrad. Spider-Man facing off against characters from The Hobbit may play well without adding up to anything like a believable shared mythology.

So reception is split. Wizards reports strong participation, while stores and longtime players describe product fatigue. A July 2026 report on a retailer poll found 42 percent calling local reaction to Marvel Super Heroes neutral, against 35 percent positive and 16 percent negative. Those numbers show the gap between commercial momentum and lasting enthusiasm.

Prices make it worse. Marvel Super Heroes Play Boosters carry a $6.99 suggested retail price, Collector Boosters $37.99, and ordinary Commander decks $74.99. Scarcity, collectible treatments, and a relentless release calendar encourage buying habits only loosely tied to playing. Wizards has made a game that rewards independent thinking and wrapped it in incentives to keep consuming. The contradiction is sharpest when a cheap, well-built deck gives better games than an expensive collection built around whatever cards are in fashion.

Magic is still an exceptional physical game, and its best qualities depend surprisingly little on its enormous product line. A well-kept collection, a regular group of opponents, an occasional draft, or a homemade cube can supply years of varied, demanding play. Its rules reward imagination and hard thinking, and its social side makes winning only part of a good evening. So the strongest criticism is not that Magic has grown too complicated or too commercial to enjoy. It is that its publisher keeps treating an inexhaustible game as if it needed inexhaustible products. Garfield made a system in which players could build something truly their own. The question facing Magic now is how much of that freedom can survive a company determined to sell them the next thing they supposedly need.
