date: 2027-02-15

Magic's rules have had two overhauls, and they pushed in opposite directions. Classic Sixth Edition in 1999 added machinery, rebuilding the timing system around the stack; Magic 2010 took machinery out, cutting the parts players kept getting wrong. Both were accused of dumbing the game down, and both gave the same answer. Everything else between the 1993 rulebook and the November 2026 Comprehensive Rules is smaller: steady cleanup, renamed terms, and rules for each new set. Today's rules in plain language are in [Magic The Gathering comprehensive rules, paraphrased](/posts/magic-the-gathering-comprehensive-rules-paraphrased/).

## The shape of it

- 1993–1996: rules by ruling. The first rulebook was short and incomplete, so the game's rules grew out of rulings on individual cards.
- 1995–1997: first attempts at a system. Fourth Edition and then Fifth Edition wrote those rulings into a framework of batches, interrupts, and damage prevention steps.
- 1999: Sixth Edition. The framework was thrown out and replaced with the stack, priority, triggered abilities, and the turn structure still used today.
- 2003–2008: building out. Layers, supertypes, keyword actions, and the planeswalker card type were added on top of the Sixth Edition base.
- 2009: Magic 2010. Fixes to the places where players' instincts were wrong: combat damage off the stack, no mana burn, and plainer words like "battlefield," "exile," and "cast."
- 2010–2026: tuning. The legend rule, mulligans, planeswalkers, and combat damage order were simplified, one set at a time.

## The early rules (1993–1994)

Limited Edition Alpha shipped in August 1993 with a short rulebook that ended with Richard Garfield's story "Worzel's Tale." Beta cut the story to make room for clarifications. The game had no mulligan, because some of the original playtesters thought a mulligan would reward bad deckbuilding. Players invented their own until the DCI adopted the first official rule in 1994. That rule was "all land or no land": a player whose opening seven held no lands or nothing but lands could reveal it and draw a new seven, once per game.

Revised Edition (April 1994) standardized card wording and introduced the tilted tap symbol. Timing was still informal. Spells were "fast effects" or not, interrupts were faster than instants, and much of how the game worked lived in rulings and in the Usenet rules FAQ, not in any rulebook.

## Fourth Edition (1995): the first system

Fourth Edition's Pocket Players' Guide was the first attempt to turn those rulings into a coherent system. Its main pieces:

- Batches. Instants and other fast effects were played in batches and resolved last in, first out. Damage dealt during a batch waited until the whole batch had resolved.
- The damage prevention step. After damage or a destroy effect resolved, there was a separate window for preventing or redirecting damage and for regenerating. A creature that had taken lethal damage was "on the way to the graveyard" until that window closed.
- Interrupts. Interrupts were their own speed, played during the casting of a spell to counter or change it.
- Odd edges. "At end of turn" effects happened before "until end of turn" effects wore off, and noncreature artifacts "turned off" while tapped.

## Fifth Edition rules (1996–1997)

The Fifth Edition rules took effect in tournaments on November 1, 1996, when Mirage became legal, months before the Fifth Edition set itself came out in March 1997. They shipped in Mirage starter decks so new players learned them first. Tom Wylie's October 1996 column in _The Duelist_ summed up the aim in its title, "Leaner and Meaner": cut what was confusing, counterintuitive, or a waste of time.

- Play or draw. The player who goes first skips their first draw. This had been an optional tournament rule; Fifth Edition made it standard.
- Mana sources. Tapping a land for mana, and other abilities that produced mana like interrupts, became "mana sources," which nothing could respond to. You could no longer Rust a Mox: Rust, which countered an artifact's ability, could not stop a Mox from making mana.
- Mana burn. Mana burn became loss of life rather than damage, so it could not be prevented or redirected.
- Costs before the colon. Anything could now be a cost: paying life, sacrificing, tapping other permanents. Greed became "{B}, Pay 2 life: Draw a card."
- The end of the turn. The end phase and the heal-creatures phase merged into a single cleanup, and "at end of turn" effects moved to the very end of the turn.
- Damage prevention without delay. A damage prevention step followed each damage-dealing effect rather than the end of a batch, and damage was grouped into "packets" that prevention and redirection effects targeted.
- New terms. Combat damage, generic mana, legendary artifacts and enchantments, local and global enchantments, and protection from anything, not only from colors.

The **Paris mulligan** arrived in 1997. Matt Hyra, a Pro Tour player who later joined Wizards, suggested it. It was tested at Pro Tour Los Angeles and then, by accident, included in the tournament rules for Pro Tour Paris in April 1997, and the name stuck. You could shuffle away any hand, without showing it, and draw a new one with one fewer card, as many times as you liked. It stayed the rule for eighteen years.

## Classic Sixth Edition (1999): the great rewrite

Classic Sixth Edition (April 21, 1999) is the largest rules change in the game's history. The rules and the card set shared a name and a release, but they were separate products: the new rules covered all of Magic, not just the core set. They took effect in tournaments on June 1, 1999.

### Who and why

Bill Rose, then Magic's lead designer, led the work. The rules team included Beth Moursund, Tom Wylie, and Richard Garfield inside Wizards, and outside rules experts including Dan Gray, Stephen D'Angelo, and Paul Barclay. The changes leaked early on the Dojo, a fan site, and Rose answered the uproar with an open letter on January 15, 1999. His case was that the Fifth Edition rules were "needlessly complicated and growing more so each month," with perhaps twenty-five real rules experts in the world and Fifth Edition questions that had no answer. "Memorizing a complex rules system" should not be what separates good players from great ones.

### What changed

- The stack. Batches and series were gone. Every spell and ability went on the stack, and players passed priority back and forth; when both passed in succession, the top object resolved. The team called it the "Grand Unified Timing System": activated abilities, instants, damage prevention, and triggered abilities all worked the same way, and anything could be responded to.
- No more interrupts. Every interrupt became an instant. Counterspell could now be answered like anything else. Rose asked readers to think of the last time it had mattered that Counterspell was an interrupt.
- Mana abilities. Mana sources were replaced by mana abilities, which still skip the stack. Mana _spells_ such as Dark Ritual became ordinary instants that can be countered.
- No damage prevention step. Damage was simply dealt when its source resolved. Under the old rules, Rose wrote, Lightning Bolt didn't deal damage; it assigned it, and the damage waited "in Never-Never Land" for a prevention step. Now you answer the Bolt with Healing Salve, and the Salve resolves first and leaves a shield. All prevention became targeted, so Healing Salve could no longer be cast on a creature with protection from white. Regeneration became a shield too.
- Triggered abilities. Any ability beginning with "when," "whenever," or "at" became a triggered ability that goes on the stack. This fixed a confusing gap: you could Unsummon a creature in response to Terror, but not in response to Nekrataal's ability. Upkeep and other phase abilities became "at the beginning of" triggers.
- The turn as it still is. Five phases: beginning (untap, upkeep, draw), main, combat, a second main phase, and end (end of turn, cleanup). Combat got its five steps: beginning of combat, declare attackers, declare blockers, combat damage, and end of combat.
- Damage on the stack. With no prevention step, combat damage was assigned and then put on the stack, where players could respond before it was dealt. It was dealt even if the creatures had left play, so a creature could deal its damage and then be sacrificed or bounced before the damage resolved. Tapped blockers now dealt combat damage too.
- Smaller fixes. A player at 0 life lost immediately, not at the end of the phase. (Rose apologized to one person, Mark Rosewater, whose puzzles depended on the old rule.) Tapped artifacts no longer turned off; cards that needed the effect, like Howling Mine, got it in their text. Modes and targets were chosen on casting, and other choices on resolution.

### Reaction and legacy

Many players feared the game was being "dumbed down," and Rose's letter argued at length that it was not, pointing to a 20,000-word rulebook then in editing. The real effect was the reverse: simpler rules, deeper play. Damage on the stack became a skill of its own for the next ten years. Mana burn nearly died in this rewrite too; Mark Rosewater argued to keep it, and won. The Comprehensive Rules that judges use today descend directly from the Sixth Edition document, and the turn is still the one it laid out.

## Building on the stack (2000–2008)

The ten years after Sixth Edition added structure without changing the core.

- Onslaught (2002): morph and face-down rules.
- Eighth Edition (2003): the "basic" supertype and land types as subtypes; formal terms "object" and "copiable values"; subgames; the draw step's draw became a turn-based action instead of a trigger on the stack.
- Mirrodin (2003): the layer system for continuous effects (copy, control, text, type, a catch-all, then power and toughness), plus Equipment and the first official list of creature types.
- Champions of Kamigawa (2004): the Legend creature type became the "legendary" supertype; Walls lost their built-in inability to attack and got the defender keyword; the snow-covered and world supertypes.
- Saviors of Kamigawa and Ninth Edition (2005): "Enchant creature" type lines gave way to the Aura subtype and the enchant keyword, and the first formal multiplayer rules arrived.
- Ravnica (2005): hybrid mana and the power-and-toughness sublayers.
- Time Spiral block (2006–2007): flash and split second; +1/+1 and -1/-1 counters cancel out. Future Sight let power, toughness, and life totals go negative instead of counting as zero.
- Tenth Edition (2007): the first update bulletin explaining changes in advance, and a free first mulligan in every multiplayer game.
- Lorwyn (2007): the planeswalker card type and the Grand Creature Type Update, which changed the types of 1,197 cards and removed oddities like "Lady-of-Proper-Etiquette."

## Magic 2010 (2009): fixing intuition

Magic 2010 (July 17, 2009) carried the second-largest rules change, and the only one since Sixth Edition with a comparable reach. The changes took effect on July 11, 2009, the first day of prerelease events.

### Who and why

Aaron Forsythe, then director of Magic R&D and the set's lead designer, announced the changes on June 10, 2009, with rules manager Mark Gottlieb writing the technical sections. Forsythe set the scale himself: the Sixth Edition changes "were meant to bring order to a disordered system," and this time the goal was "much more subtle—to change the most unintuitive parts of game play such that players' first instincts were more often correct." Most Magic is played without a rulebook. Players who learn a kitchen-table version should not find out at Friday Night Magic that they have been doing it all wrong.

### Terminology

- "In play" became **the battlefield**: permanents "enter the battlefield" instead of "coming into play."
- "Remove from the game" became **exile**. Because exile is in the game, the Wishes could no longer fetch exiled cards from "outside the game."
- "Play" split into **cast** (spells), **activate** (abilities), and **play** (lands).
- The end of turn step became the **end step**, and "at end of turn" became "at the beginning of the end step," closing the "end-of-turn loophole," in which a creature put onto the battlefield after the end-of-turn triggers had passed escaped its "at end of turn, sacrifice it" clause.
- State-based effects became **state-based actions**, and game actions became **turn-based actions**.

### Rules

- Combat damage leaves the stack. Damage is dealt as soon as it is assigned, with no window to respond. Forsythe listed the confusions it ended: how is that Mogg Fanatic killing two creatures, how did that creature kill mine and still make your Nantuko Husk big enough to survive? Combat tricks now had to be played in the declare blockers step.
- Damage assignment order. To keep double blocks fair without that window, the attacker ordered multiple blockers when blocks were declared and had to assign lethal damage to each in turn before moving to the next.
- Mana burn removed. Gottlieb found that deleting it took three sentences from rule 300.3. Mana pools now empty at the end of every step, not just every phase. About forty cards changed in power and none got errata. "In 99.9% of Magic games, of course, you'll never even notice mana burn is gone." Rosewater, who had saved mana burn in 1999, argued for its removal this time: it confused new players and cost design space without earning its place.
- Deathtouch and lifelink became static abilities instead of triggers, so multiple instances stopped stacking.
- Simultaneous mulligans. Players announce in turn order, then everyone mulliganing does it at once.
- Token ownership. A token's owner became the player under whose control it entered, not whoever controlled the effect that made it.
- The rulebook reorganized into the nine sections still used today, from Game Concepts to Multiplayer, and the layers were split into seven.

### Reaction and legacy

Competitive players' first reaction was mixed to negative. They felt removing damage on the stack "dumbed down" the game, and a few cards, such as Citadel of Pain, stopped doing anything. Forsythe later admitted the deathtouch change had been rushed, and Magic 2011 revised it so deathtouch damage could be spread efficiently across multiple blockers and combine with trample. The words stuck, though: "battlefield," "exile," and "cast" are now so ordinary that the old terms read like history.

## Tuning (2010–2023)

- Magic 2011 (2010): deathtouch revised; emblems added.
- Mirrodin Besieged (2011): "EDH" became Commander in the rules, and "general" became "commander," with color identity defined.
- Innistrad (2011): double-faced cards and color indicators.
- Magic 2014 (2013): the **legend rule** changed. Before, two copies of the same legend on the battlefield, under any players' control, all went to the graveyard; now only your own copies count, and you keep one. The same change came to the planeswalker uniqueness rule. Indestructible became a keyword ability, "unblockable" was retired in favor of "can't be blocked," and sideboards could take up to fifteen cards with no one-for-one swaps.
- Battle for Zendikar (2015): the **Vancouver mulligan**, tested at Pro Tour Magic Origins in Vancouver, added a scry 1 after taking a mulligan.
- Oath of the Gatewatch (2016): the colorless mana symbol {C}.
- Amonkhet (2017): split cards combine both halves' characteristics outside the stack.
- Ixalan (2017): the planeswalker uniqueness rule was removed, and every planeswalker became legendary.
- Dominaria (2018): the **planeswalker redirection rule** was removed; damage spells now say "any target." "Add {G} to your mana pool" shortened to "Add {G}," and rules text adopted the singular "they."
- Core Set 2019 (2018): a token's owner became the player who created it.
- War of the Spark (2019): proliferate adds one counter of each kind already present.
- Core Set 2020 (2019): the **London mulligan**, tested at Mythic Championship London: draw seven every time, then put one card on the bottom for each mulligan.
- Companion (June 2020): after companion proved too strong, getting it into your hand became a three-mana special action at sorcery speed.
- Core Set 2021 (2020): a commander can go to its graveyard or exile and return to the command zone as a state-based action later.
- Strixhaven (2021): "converted mana cost" became **mana value**.
- March of the Machine (2023): the battle card type.

## The recent era (2024–2026)

- Bloomburrow (2024): "enters the battlefield" shortened to **"enters,"** and "precombat" and "postcombat" main phase became "first" and "second."
- Commander (September 2024): the Commander Rules Committee dissolved and handed the format to Wizards; later updates moved its rulings into the Comprehensive Rules.
- Foundations (November 2024): **damage assignment order was removed.** The Magic 2010 ordering step is gone; an attacker blocked by several creatures divides its damage among them as its controller chooses when damage is dealt. Cards also mostly stopped naming themselves in their text ("this creature").
- Marvel's Spider-Man (2025): modal double-faced cards can transform.
- Edge of Eternities (2025): Vehicles and Spacecraft with power and toughness can be commanders.
- Secrets of Strixhaven (2026): Sagas add their first lore counter through an intrinsic ability, as planeswalkers do with loyalty, and preparation cards were introduced.
- Star Trek (November 2026): meld cards are no longer double-faced cards, and the double-faced card rules were reorganized.

## What the two overhauls share

Sixth Edition and Magic 2010 came ten years apart and pushed in opposite directions. Sixth Edition added machinery—the stack, priority, damage on the stack—to replace a pile of special cases with one system. Magic 2010 removed machinery that the system made possible but players did not expect. Both faced the same charge of "dumbing down," and both answered it the same way: the skill should be in the cards and the play, not in memorizing the rules. Since Magic 2010, the pattern has held: when players' first instinct keeps being wrong, the rule changes. The 2024 removal of damage assignment order is the latest step on that line, undoing part of Magic 2010 itself.
