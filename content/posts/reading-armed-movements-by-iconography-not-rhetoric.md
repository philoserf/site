---
title: Reading armed movements by iconography, not rhetoric
description: Uniforms, torchlight marches, and leader portraits reveal when an armed anti-system faction shifts from dissent to seizing power, since rhetoric is universal and cheap. Charlottesville 2017 and the January 30, 1933 Brandenburg Gate march illustrate the shift.
date: 2026-10-26
aliases:
  - Uniforms as a Warning Sign
---

Self-description in politics is worthless as evidence. Almost every faction that has seized power by force did so in the name of liberation, restoration or defense against a corrupt system, so a movement's claim to be against the system is not a warning sign or a reassurance. It is the universal preamble. What tells you something is what the movement does with the instruments of organized power once it has them, and there is a moment when that becomes visible to anyone standing on the sidewalk.

A line that circulates online puts it this way: men with guns who have always claimed to be against the system are most dangerous once they start wearing uniforms and marching with torches and pictures of a leader. [Source if it has one.] The observation is better than its apocalyptic tail, and it repays taking seriously as a test.

Consider what each element does. Men who claim to oppose the system take their identity and their moral license from dissent; their guns are, in their own telling, defensive, held in reserve against tyranny. The costume change ends that story. Uniforms replace the improvised dress of the irregular and announce hierarchy, discipline, and the intention to constitute an order rather than resist one. Torches are for being seen at night by people meant to feel afraid. The picture of a leader collapses a movement that claimed to be about principle into one organized around a person. Together they convert anti-system rhetoric into system-building of a particular kind: the rule of a faction and its chief.

The men who marched through Charlottesville in August 2017 carried torches and chanted a slogan from the Third Reich, and the chants were the least informative thing about them. Their predecessors marched through the Brandenburg Gate on the night of January 30th, 1933, and the torchlight then was not a protest either. It was advance notice. An armed faction that opposes a system stays, however uncomfortably, in a stance of restraint. The march announces that the restraint is ending: the movement is no longer asking to be heard, it is rehearsing its authority in public, performing the aesthetics of a regime before it is one, and telling the surrounding population that whoever promised to guard against tyranny now intends to administer it.

This is why the test works where listening fails. Rhetoric is cheap and reusable; a uniform, a chain of command and a leader's face are public commitments, and you cannot wear them on Saturday and be merely against the system on Monday. Watch the iconography rather than the manifestos. A movement will say what such movements always say, right up until the morning it stops looking like a protest and starts looking like an army with a face on its banner.
