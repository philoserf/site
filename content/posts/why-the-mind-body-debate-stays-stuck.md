---
title: Why the mind-body debate stays stuck
description: Materialism and idealism each solve what the other cannot, trapped by a shared premise that reality must have one fundamental category; a pragmatic, coordinate-system approach treats them as complementary descriptions rather than competing truths.
date: 2026-12-21
---

The two dominant answers to the mind-body problem—materialism and idealism—are each more serious than the other's partisans admit, and each fails at the exact point where the other begins. Materialism can't get experience out of matter, and idealism can't get individuation and structure out of experience. That symmetry is the signature of a premise both sides share, and the premise, more than either side's arguments, keeps the debate stuck.

Materialism's case is strong. It rests on causal closure, a premise materialists bring to physics rather than a finding of it: every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and there is no empirical evidence of non-physical forces intervening in brain activity. The massive success of neuroscience in correlating mental states with brain states reinforces it; lesion studies, neuroimaging, pharmacology, and stimulation experiments all show mind tracking matter with remarkable consistency. Parsimony helps too: if physical explanations suffice, positing additional ontological categories looks unmotivated. A majority of philosophers surveyed in 2009 accepted or leaned toward physicalism, and materialism deserves its standing as the default.

Idealism's case is equally genuine, and it is epistemological: experience is the only thing anyone encounters directly. Every physical object, every scientific measurement, every brain scan is known to you only as a structured appearance within consciousness. Matter, on this view, is a theoretical posit—useful, powerful, spectacularly successful, but still an abstraction built on top of the one thing you actually have. Berkeley made the point, but it has outlived him: no one has refuted it, only set it aside as inconvenient. Kant took it seriously enough to build transcendental idealism around it, while rejecting Berkeley's version outright. The philosopher Bernardo Kastrup presses it today: matter, materialism's foundation, is something we never access except through the medium it is supposed to explain.

Each position has a signature problem it cannot solve, and the two are mirror images. Materialism faces what David Chalmers named the hard problem of consciousness: why should any physical process be accompanied by subjective experience at all? The responses each trade one problem for another. Illusionists such as Keith Frankish ask you to accept that the most certain thing in your experience, that there is something it is like to be you, is a kind of error. Identity theorists say pain is a particular kind of nerve firing, which names the correlation without explaining why that process feels like anything.

Idealism faces the combination problem: if consciousness is fundamental, why does it come divided into billions of separate subjects, why do their boundaries match biological organisms, and why does damage to a brain alter or end one? Kastrup answers with a metaphor—universal consciousness dissociates into individual minds the way a person with dissociative identity disorder fragments into alters—but metaphor is not entailment. Alfred North Whitehead's process metaphysics is more rigorous but buys its answer with a thicket of new categories that touch empirical science almost nowhere, and the cognitive scientist Donald Hoffman's network of conscious agents does the same. Every answer either smuggles in physical-structural facts, at which point something like materialism does the explanatory work, or posits mechanisms no experiment could test. Neither problem is unsolved the way protein folding was; in both cases no one can say what a solution would look like.

The remaining arguments extend the mirror. Hempel's dilemma catches materialism: if "physical" means current physics, materialism is almost certainly false, because current physics is incomplete; if it means a future completed physics, the claim is empty, because no one knows what that physics will contain. Idealism has the matching weakness: our best explanations of why experience takes the forms it does—why you see colors, feel pain, remember your childhood—are all physical-structural, and until idealism can generate its own, it remains parasitic on the framework it claims to supersede. Neuroscience's correlations establish dependence, not identity, so they fit property dualism and neutral monism as well as materialism. Parsimony, which both sides claim, cancels out: each position is simple on one side and profligate on the other. And Colin McGinn's proposal that we are constitutionally unable to grasp how matter makes consciousness saves materialism only by making it unverifiable, an odd position for a view that prides itself on empirical grounding.

Why is there a stalemate at all? The standard reading is that the problem is hard and we haven't found the answer yet. The symmetry says something else. Both positions assume that the mind-body problem is a problem of reduction: one side must be derived from the other, and whichever side you derive from is the real one. Materialism tries to get mind out of matter; idealism tries to get matter out of mind. Both fail, and the premise that reality has a single fundamental category goes unquestioned. That premise does most of the damage.

Physics has dropped premises like this before. General relativity replaced gravitational force with spacetime geometry. Quantum field theory replaced particles with excitations of fields. In both cases "what is it really made of?" turned out to be less useful than "what are the structural relations?"

The obvious exits don't take that lesson; they swap one fundamental for another. Relational views make relation fundamental and the things related derivative: structural realism in philosophy of physics, enactivism in cognitive science, dependent origination in Buddhist philosophy. Process views, Whitehead's above all, make events fundamental and treat matter and experience alike as abstractions from them. Neutral monism posits a third stuff of which mind and matter are aspects. Each deserves a hearing, and each keeps the premise: name the one thing reality is made of, then derive the rest. All three will meet their own version of the mirror problem, whether that is how relations yield a subject or how events yield the felt unity of a moment.

The exit that drops the premise is pragmatic. Both positions proceed as if there is a fact of the matter about which category is "really" fundamental, independent of what you are trying to do. But choosing between them is more like choosing a coordinate system than discovering a brute fact. Cartesian and polar coordinates describe the same plane; neither is more real. Materialism is the right coordinate system for neuroscience. Something closer to idealism is the right one for phenomenology and contemplative practice, where experience is the datum. The mistake is thinking you have to pick one for everything.

Polar coordinates break down at the origin: the angle there is undefined, though nothing is wrong with the plane. The hard problem and the combination problem look like the same kind of breakdown, places where one description cannot express what the other takes as basic. The fault lies in the descriptions; the plane is whole. And the subject-object boundary each position takes as given—objects first for materialism, subjects first for idealism—looks less like a discovery than like a choice of origin.

This is no more relativism than coordinate geometry is. Distances, angles, and the shape of the plane survive any change of coordinates, and the pragmatist is committed to whatever the two descriptions agree on: that brain damage changes minds, that experience has lawful structure, that the correlations neuroscience finds are real. What retires is the question of which description is the real one.

Nothing changes in the lab. Neuroscientists keep working in physical coordinates, as they do now. What changes is the question the rest of us bring to their work. "Am I just my brain?" stops being a demand to choose a substance and becomes a question about which description serves which purpose: the scan for the surgeon, the experience for the person living it.

If a future framework reconceives the relation between physical structure and experience so that the hard problem and the combination problem stop looking like problems—the way special relativity dissolved the question of what medium light travels through—then neither materialism nor idealism will be vindicated. Both will be superseded by a view that shows the debate was badly framed. Dissolution is not closure; it admits the question was wrong, which is a different kind of resolution. And it is the only kind available for a question whose terms guarantee that no answer given within them is complete.
