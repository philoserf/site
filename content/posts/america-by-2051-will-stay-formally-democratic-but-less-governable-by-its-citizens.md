---
title: America by 2051 will stay formally democratic but less governable by its citizens
description: Union membership fell from 20.1% in 1983 to 10% in 2025, and 17% of Americans trust Washington to do what is right. They will still vote in 2051. Will their votes still change how they are governed?
date: 2026-12-03
---

By 2051, I expect an America that is still wealthy, still technologically formidable, and still holding elections, but that offers increasingly unequal chances to influence government or escape its failures: a recognizable United States, with more concentrated power and wider differences in lived freedom across states and classes.

Looking back fifty years, I see wider inclusion alongside weaker collective leverage. Many people gained opportunities and recognition unavailable in 1976, while some of the institutions through which ordinary people exercised sustained power weakened. Union membership among wage and salary workers fell from 20.1% in 1983 to 10% in 2025.

The economic record resists a simple story of decline. Pew finds that the middle-income share of Americans fell from 61% in 1971 to 51% in 2023, but more people moved up than down. Prosperity and security are different things, though, and neither automatically gives citizens an effective voice. A country can grow richer while its people feel its essential institutions slipping beyond their control. Trust shows the estrangement: in Pew's series it has stayed below 30% since 2007, and in September 2025 only 17% trusted the federal government to do what is right most or almost all of the time.

We have spent decades building a dangerous mismatch: people are promised political equality and meet institutions they struggle to influence. Demagogues can exploit that experience, and assuring citizens that the institutions are sound will not resolve it. The deterioration is now measurable. V-Dem's 2026 assessment records a sharp American decline in constraints on executive power, civil liberties, and free expression during 2025, while its election measures still largely reflected the 2024 vote. Institutional erosion is not yet the loss of electoral competition.

Looking ahead, I expect four developments.

The presidency will remain the main prize in a struggle over concentrated power. Unless Congress rebuilds its capacity and willingness to govern, each administration will keep the powers it inherited, and a change of party will interrupt particular abuses without reversing the accumulation.

Citizenship will grow more unequal by geography. States will diverge further in public services, labor protections, education, and the practical exercise of rights, while federal power stays strong enough to intrude on them. People with resources will choose where they live; others will bear decisions they cannot escape.

Technological abundance may coexist with dependence. AI could make expertise, medicine, and education far more accessible, or strengthen surveillance and the firms that control essential infrastructure. Ownership, competition, and bargaining rights will decide whether productivity turns into independence for ordinary people.

Fiscal pressure will sharpen the fight over distribution. An aging population and rising health and interest costs force the question of whose benefits, taxes, and security are negotiable and whose are protected. Debt does not dictate an authoritarian outcome, but the conflict over adjustment can open the way to one.

My central scenario, then, is a country where elections still sometimes change governments but ordinary citizens struggle to change the arrangements that govern their lives. Many would live comfortably within it; others would face arbitrary authority and persistent insecurity. Such an order could last a long time. A harder authoritarian turn is plausible, with opposition nominally legal but facing selective enforcement and intimidation. So is democratic renewal, which would take durable organizations and reforms that distribute practical power at work, in communities, and in government. Anger alone could feed either.

I would grow more optimistic if electoral victories led to enforceable limits on executive power, stronger independent institutions, and broader economic security, and more pessimistic if opposition victories stopped translating into governing authority.

In twenty-five years, I expect Americans will still vote. What I am less sure of is whether those votes will give them real power to change how they are governed.
