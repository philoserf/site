---
title: Critique of the tripartite moral self
description: Revisits the author's own claim that humans consist of a self, an external benignant angel, and an embedded evil, against Hermas, Paul, and Augustine. Argues the model captures moral phenomenology but fails as anatomy—it leaves the self an empty chooser, ignores how practice brings the good inside, and misplaces where good and evil come from.
date: 2026-11-23
---

I once wrote that "A human is a tripartite creature. The human itself, some angelic external and benignant, and some evil, also embedded there," and left the sentence unfinished, its closing comma promising a clause I never supplied. It proposes a moral anatomy in which a person is identical neither to their better impulses nor to their worse ones, standing instead between them as a separate party. The arrangement is more lopsided than the word "tripartite" suggests.

The two non-human elements are not placed symmetrically. The angelic presence is "external and benignant"; the evil is "embedded there." The word "also" hedges, as if I began to say that both were embedded and then gave the good an outside address anyway. The strongest reading takes the asymmetry at face value: goodness visits, evil resides. This sets the claim apart from its nearest traditional relative, the early Christian Shepherd of Hermas, which gives every person two attending angels, one of righteousness and one of wickedness, both companions at the same distance. The statement moves the wicked one indoors. In doing so it sits closer to Paul in Romans 7, who separates the "I" from a sin that "dwells within me," and who concludes that the evil he does is not really his doing.

Augustine supplies the other half. He treats the will as ruined by sin without reducing the person to the ruin, and insists that grace must come from God rather than be manufactured by unaided willing. Paul's indwelling sin and Augustine's grace from beyond together give the statement its shape: evil at home, good on the way in.

The arrangement captures the texture of moral experience. Temptation rarely feels foreign. It speaks in one's own voice, draws on one's own appetites, and presents itself as simply what one wants. The good, by contrast, often arrives as an interruption: a demand, a sense of being addressed, a claim that reaches the person from somewhere beyond present desire. Many who have resisted something they badly wanted will recognize the feeling of being on the receiving end of the better impulse rather than its author. The separate "human itself" adds a further detail, the sense of watching both pressures and having to consent to one of them. As phenomenology, the model is accurate.

As anatomy it fails, and the failure starts with the third term. If the good lives outside and the evil lives inside, the human itself contributes nothing but the act of choosing. A chooser with no dispositions of its own has no grounds for choosing, so its choices are either arbitrary or secretly determined by whichever occupant pushes harder. The self the statement set out to honor dissolves into a switchboard.

Nor can the model account for moral development. Aristotle's observation that people become just by doing just acts has no place in a scheme where goodness stays permanently outside. Someone who has told the truth for forty years does not experience honesty as a visiting angel; it has become second nature, part of who they are.

The statement is right that the good often starts outside. Much of what becomes an inner voice began there. Other people correct a person. Traditions teach standards no one invented alone. Literature enlarges sympathies, and friendship calls forth loyalties that solitary reflection might never produce. Religious language calls such interventions grace; secular language calls them education, example, or inheritance. Either way, no one invents the whole vocabulary by which they judge themselves. But what arrives from outside is exactly what practice brings inside: the correction becomes a habit, and the habit becomes character. The statement fixes the good at a distance that a lived moral life steadily closes.

The geometry fails as a map of motivation, too. Attachment, empathy, parental care, and the capacity for self-sacrifice are as native to human beings as aggression; an animal this cooperative could not have evolved without them. Evil arrives from outside as readily as good does, since cruelty can be taught, rewarded, bureaucratized, and imposed by institutions.

The statement assumes, too, that the two occupants can be cleanly labeled. Anger at cruelty is embedded, appetitive, and often exactly right, while a benevolent-seeming external voice can be the internalized approval of a group whose standards deserve no loyalty. Impulses rarely announce which of the two they belong to, and much moral work consists of sorting them.

The statement is a faithful picture of the beginning of moral life and a false picture of its structure. The good feels external in proportion to how little it has been practiced, and evil feels embedded in proportion to how much appetite precedes reflection. Both conditions are real, and both are temporary in anyone who is changing. A theory that fixes the addresses of good and evil cannot register the movement between them, and that movement is where most of ethics happens.

The one thing the statement gets more right than its Pauline ancestor is its refusal to disown the evil. Separating the self from its impulses has a real use—a person who can imagine violence need not identify as violent—but Paul's "it is no longer I who do it" converts indwelling sin into a tenant for whom the landlord bears no responsibility, and that move has licensed a long history of self-exculpation. By calling evil embedded and keeping it inside the creature's own definition, the statement denies that exit. The same logic, applied honestly, should have forced the good inside as well, because what one is answerable for cannot be limited to what one resists; it must include what one has become.
