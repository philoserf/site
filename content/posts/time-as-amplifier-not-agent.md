---
title: Time as amplifier, not agent
date: 2027-02-01
description: Wounds close, wine deepens, and grief turns survivable; the saying goes that time does the work. Yet the untreated cancer and the unwritten manuscript run on the same clock. So what is time doing?
---

The proposition that time does a lot of work has force. Erosion grinds mountains into plains; wounds close while we sleep; grief, which on the day of loss feels permanent, becomes by some quiet arithmetic survivable. Wine deepens in the barrel, capital compounds in the account, children grow through nights when no one is paying attention. Science, Planck observed, advances one funeral at a time—opinions that argument cannot dislodge are eventually carried off by mortality. Noticing this is a kind of wisdom, because much of human anxiety consists in trying to force outcomes that would arrive on their own if left undisturbed.

The opposing case is that time, considered apart from what happens within it, does no work at all. Time is a measure; to credit it with accomplishment is to credit the calendar for the harvest. The work belongs to biological, chemical, geological, and social processes; time is merely the medium in which they unfold. The proposition also has a corrupted reading that nearly everyone has lived through: the procrastinator's faith that putting something off is the same as solving it. Untreated cancers metastasize. Unfinished conversations harden into estrangements. Skills decay, debts compound in the wrong direction, the manuscript does not write itself. Left alone, the processes still run, and many of them run downhill. The cases where time appears to do good work are partly a selection effect: the ruined marriages and squandered talents do not write thank-you notes to the years that finished them off.

The two readings reconcile once time is seen for what it is: not an agent but an amplifier. It magnifies whatever processes are already running—generative or corrosive, intentional or neglected. Plant well and wait, and time does extraordinary work; plant nothing or plant poorly, and time still does extraordinary work, just not the kind anyone wanted. The aphorism is true and useful as a corrective to the modern presumption that everything worthwhile must be hustled into existence; it is dangerous as a substitute for the effort that gives time something worth amplifying.
