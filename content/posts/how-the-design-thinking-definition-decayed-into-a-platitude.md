title: How the Design Thinking definition decayed into a platitude
description: A once-specific definition of Design Thinking as "linking ideas and methods from different areas into new combinations" now excludes nothing and functions as a legitimating platitude, tracked against critiques by Natasha Jen, Lee Vinsel, and Maggie Gram.
date: 2026-11-09

  - Design Thinking's Definition as Platitude
---

When Design Thinking was a young movement—the era of the Stanford d.school, IDEO's ascendancy, Tim Brown's 2008 essay announcing it to managers—a description of it as something that "links smart ideas and practical methods from different areas together, and creates new combinations" did real work. It pointed. There was a recognizable practice it was trying to name: a way of treating designerly habits, prototyping, user observation, iteration, as transferable beyond product styling and importable into strategy, services, and organizational problems. The sentence felt true partly because it felt specific. It distinguished a new thing from the older, narrower sense of design as the shaping of objects.

The sentence no longer distinguishes anything. Linking ideas and methods from different fields and producing new combinations is not a description of Design Thinking; it is a description of engineering, of scientific synthesis, of cooking, of jazz, of essentially any creative or intellectual activity that is going well. A definition earns its keep by excluding—by telling you what would not count. This one excludes nothing. What read as specificity in 2005 reads as universality in 2025, and a universal definition is functionally an empty one.

The statement has changed genre without changing wording. It began as something like a hypothesis or a description: a claim about a real, bounded practice that could be true or false. It now operates as boilerplate, the kind of sentence that survives in brochures, course catalogs, and consulting decks because it is agreeable rather than informative. Its truth conditions have loosened so far that whether it is true is no longer the interesting question. It is the sort of thing nobody disputes and nobody learns from.

This drift tracks Design Thinking's history. The practice ran a full hype cycle. The expansive promise of the 2000s met, in the late 2010s, a wave of pointed criticism—Natasha Jen's "Design Thinking is Bullshit," Lee Vinsel's argument that it had become a content-free brand, Maggie Gram's account of how the industry repackaged a craft tradition and sold it as a portable methodology. The charge in each was roughly the same: that Design Thinking, scaled and franchised, had thinned into a set of sticky-note rituals and reassuring abstractions detached from the discipline and constraint that made design work in the first place. A sentence praising the "linking" of "smart ideas" and the creation of "new combinations" is, read against that critique, almost a specimen of it. It performs the very vagueness at issue, in the frictionless register the critics were objecting to.

Fairly classified, it is the wrong kind of statement to interrogate for truth. Better to file it as a platitude, or more precisely a legitimating formula: a sentence whose present function is social rather than epistemic. It signals affiliation with a respected practice and reassures an audience; it does not transmit a testable claim or pick out a referent. That is a real and common category—plenty of once-substantive ideas end up there. The honest move is to demote the sentence accordingly: keep it, if at all, as period rhetoric, and reach for a sharper account if you actually want to say what Design Thinking is or whether it works. A definition that fits everything has stopped being a definition and become a mood.
