---
title: Crisis-driven patchwork versus optimal design
description: Critique of the claim that systems evolve through crisis-driven patchwork rather than optimal design, arguing the contrast is rigged against a strawman, conflates trigger with mode, ignores survivorship bias, and risks becoming an alibi for neglecting foresight.
date: 2026-09-23
aliases:
  - The Myth of Patchwork Inevitability
---

Fire escapes followed fires, deposit insurance followed bank runs, and the modern building code reads as a ledger of past disasters rewritten in the grammar of prevention. If the realistic trajectory is crisis-driven patchwork rather than optimal design, the evidence is everywhere a person cares to look. Tax law accretes rather than evolves; financial regulation arrives one crash late; the protocols carrying this sentence across the internet were never planned so much as repeatedly amended around the failures of their predecessors. The standard story of how large systems acquire their shape is one of damage, response, and the hardening of that response into something nobody would have chosen from scratch but everybody now depends on.

The framing earns its descriptive force precisely because optimal design so rarely has the conditions it requires: a single agent, adequate knowledge, and the authority to act. Those conditions almost never co-occur. Most consequential systems have many authors, partial information, entrenched interests, and a running installed base that cannot be switched off while the replacement is built. Charles Lindblom called the resulting method muddling through, and the phrase is apt: actors compare a few feasible options near the status quo, adjust at the margin, and revisit later. Crisis is what makes the margin move. Absent a failure vivid enough to overcome inertia, the patch does not get applied at all.

But the contrast is rigged, and noticing how it is rigged is more useful than admiring its realism. Optimal design is a strawman; nothing in the world offers it, so anything real will look like patchwork by comparison. The honest alternative to crisis-driven fixes is ordinary competent foresight—decent planning, maintained over time, that heads off some failures before they become emergencies. By measuring reality against an unattainable ideal rather than against good-enough deliberation, the claim makes patchwork look inevitable when much of it is merely chosen, or defaulted into.

The framing also conflates two things that come apart: the trigger and the mode. Crisis is a trigger; patchwork is a mode of response. They are correlated but not identical, and the most consequential episodes are precisely the ones where crisis drove something other than a patch. The Articles of Confederation failed and produced a new constitution, not a patch. The Depression produced a reordering of the relationship between state and economy, not a tweak. Postwar Germany and Japan received wholesale institutional redesigns under the pressure of total defeat. Crisis, it turns out, is often the only thing that opens a window wide enough for comprehensive design—which inverts the dichotomy. Sometimes crisis is the enemy of patchwork rather than its cause, because it discredits the jury-rigged arrangement thoroughly enough that incrementalism is no longer on the table.

A deeper objection: patchwork may not be the regrettable fallback at all. James Scott's account of high-modernist planning is a catalogue of optimal designs—planned cities, collectivized agriculture, scientific forestry—that failed catastrophically because they legislated away the local, tacit knowledge that made the messy original work. Against that record, patchwork looks less like a failure to design well and more like a robust strategy: it preserves option value, it tests each change against a real failure rather than an imagined model, and it refuses the hubris of betting everything on one plan. Evolution, the most prolific designer we know of, works exactly this way and produces the inverted retina, the recurrent laryngeal nerve, and the human spine—kludges all, and good enough to inherit the earth. Where there is no designer at all, as in markets or language or common law, patchwork is the form emergent order takes.

Some of the appearance of universal patchwork is also a selection effect. The cleanly designed systems that failed are gone, decommissioned, forgotten; the ones still standing for inspection are disproportionately the ones that survived by being patched. Observing kludge everywhere and concluding that kludge is the only realistic path confuses the survivors with the population.

What the maxim does least well is govern its own use. As description it is largely right and worth internalizing against the seductions of the grand plan. As counsel it is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is dangerous. Held one way it is humility: do not stake everything on a design you cannot validate, expect to revise, build for repair. Held another way it is an alibi: since only crisis moves anything, there is no point investing in foresight, and the failure to plan becomes self-justifying. A belief that change is only ever crisis-driven helps ensure that it is, because the people who could have acted earlier decline to, citing the very pattern their inaction sustains. The realistic trajectory is partly a product of what the actors inside it take to be possible, and a maxim that tells them patchwork is all they can expect is one of the forces bending that trajectory, not a neutral report on it.
