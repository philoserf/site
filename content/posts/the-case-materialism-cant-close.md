---
title: The Case Materialism Can't Close
description: Materialism has strong empirical support but cannot fully close the hard problem of consciousness.
date: 2026-11-11
---

The case for materialism is real but weaker than its proponents present it, and it can never be fully closed.

The strongest arguments rest on causal closure: physics appears causally complete, every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and there is no empirical evidence of non-physical forces intervening in brain activity. This is compelling. Neuroscience reinforces it by correlating mental states with brain states. Lesion studies, neuroimaging, pharmacology, and stimulation experiments all point toward mind tracking matter consistently: stimulate the right patch of cortex and the patient smells burnt toast. The parsimony argument also holds: if physical explanations suffice, positing additional ontological categories looks unmotivated. Together they make materialism the best-supported default framework in analytic philosophy and cognitive science, and it deserves that status.

But the hard problem of consciousness remains. It is a conceptual gap rather than an unsolved empirical question: why should any physical process carry subjective experience at all? Materialists have responses. Illusionism, in Keith Frankish and Daniel Dennett, asks you to accept that the most immediately certain thing in your experience—that there is something it is like to be you—is a kind of error. That is a remarkable thing to ask. Identity theory faces the explanatory gap: saying pain is C-fiber firing doesn't explain why that particular physical process has that particular qualitative character. Functionalism struggles with inverted qualia and the conceivability of philosophical zombies, which at minimum shows that the connection between function and experience isn't analytically transparent. Each of these positions trades one problem for another, and none of them closes the case.

The causal closure argument, though it holds, is subtler than it looks. It assumes the inventory of physics is complete enough to ground the claim, but physics itself keeps revising its ontology. Quantum mechanics introduced observer-dependent features that sit uneasily in a naive materialist frame. This does not vindicate dualism, but it undercuts the confidence that we know what "physical" means well enough to declare everything is physical. Hempel's dilemma sharpens this into a problem about what the materialist claim even means. If "physical" means the ontology of current physics, materialism is almost certainly false, because current physics is incomplete. If it means whatever a future completed physics posits, the claim is vacuous, because we don't know what that physics will look like. No third option makes materialism both substantive and defensible.

The empirical correlation argument is strong evidence for supervenience, for mental states depending on physical states. But supervenience is compatible with several non-materialist positions, including property dualism and neutral monism. Correlation establishes dependence, not identity. To close the case, materialism would need to show much more: that subjective experience is entailed by physical facts, that a complete physical description of the world logically necessitates consciousness. It is not clear what form such a demonstration could even take. We can imagine a completed neuroscience that maps every neural correlate of every conscious state with perfect precision. That would be an extraordinary achievement, but it would still be a correlation map. The question of why those particular arrangements produce those particular experiences, or any at all, would remain untouched, because the explanatory gap isn't empirical. It concerns the logical relationship between third-person physical descriptions and first-person phenomenal facts.

Some materialists argue the gap is merely epistemic, a limitation of our cognitive architecture rather than a feature of reality. Colin McGinn's cognitive closure hypothesis takes this line: we are constitutionally unable to grasp the physical property that makes matter conscious, the way a dog cannot grasp calculus. This is coherent, but it concedes the point in practice. If the gap can never be closed from our side, materialism becomes an article of faith about what reality is like beyond our ability to verify it, an odd position for a view that prides itself on empirical grounding.

The question may dissolve rather than get answered, which would be more modest and more interesting than closure. If a future framework reconceives the relationship between physical structure and experience in a way that makes the hard problem stop looking like a problem—the way special relativity dissolved the question of what medium carries light—then a new view supersedes materialism rather than vindicating it. Some versions of integrated information theory, neutral monism, and structural realism point this way. But dissolution is not closure. It acknowledges the question was wrong, a different kind of resolution, and one that leaves strict materialism behind along with its rivals.

The demand—deriving first-person facts from third-person descriptions—may be inherently unsatisfiable. The best materialism can achieve is continued empirical success plus the absence of a better alternative. That is a strong practical position. It is also a permanent stalemate.
