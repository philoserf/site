A Magic set you open this year began about three years ago, and it passed through more hands on the way than most players imagine. The organization inside Wizards of the Coast that makes Magic looks like a game studio, a publishing house, a software shop, and a research lab at once.

Magic has no single design team. It has several overlapping disciplines that invent, refine, test, interpret, and present the game. Wizards documents much of this in public: the designers' names, internal design documents, what each team does, and the reasoning behind individual cards.

## How the work is organized

A set moves through five design stages:

1. Exploratory Design investigates possibilities, novel mechanics, and experimental ideas.
2. Vision Design sets the set's identity, themes, and mechanical direction.
3. Set Design builds the card file, mechanics, archetypes, and play environment.
4. Competitive Play Design tests Draft, Standard, and other competitive environments.
5. Casual Play Design tests Commander, multiplayer, and casual experiences.

Alongside them, from start to finish, run worldbuilding and narrative, rules architecture and editing, art direction and visual design, product architecture and engineering, quality assurance and production, and organized play and format management.

This is a workflow, not a reporting chain. The teams overlap, and creative and rules specialists join well before production begins.

In August 2026, Mark Rosewater explained that designing and playtesting a set takes about two and a half years, followed by roughly six months of production and distribution. The pipeline runs about three years ahead of release.

The vocabulary has changed over the years. In the Urza and Masques era, the split was simply _Design_ and _Development_. Today's division into exploratory, vision, set, and play design came out of decades of organizational learning.

The roles cover corporate leadership, game design, creative direction, and player experience.

Rosewater carries unusual weight. He joined Wizards in 1995 and has become the main public voice of Magic's design philosophy, with influence reaching from individual mechanics to the color pie, complexity management, innovation, and the design process itself. Forsythe works at a broader organizational level. The two do different jobs: Rosewater guards the game's design principles, and Forsythe helps run the larger enterprise that puts them into practice.

They work in rotating, cross-functional teams: one designer leads a vision team, another drops in to check Limited balance, a third later replaces much of the original card file. The same people often work on several sets at once, each at a different stage.

### The play designers

Play Design brings competitive experience and systematic playtesting into the process:

- Andrew Brown is technical lead for Play Design and was set design lead for _Edge of Eternities_.
- Jadine Klomparens is Magic's lead play designer, working on each Standard-legal set.
- Ben Weitz works across play design and set design.
- Carmen Klomparens is a senior game designer on the Play Design team, working on set development and format management.
- Melissa DeTora leads Casual Play Design, including Commander playtesting.

They matter because a design can succeed aesthetically and mechanically and still fail competitively. Play Design looks at what happens once players start optimizing.

Wizards runs two testing environments. The **Future Future League (FFL)** simulates future Standard metagames with unreleased cards: the team builds decks, explores combinations, and tries to find what competitive players will find. The **Casual Play League (CPL)** tests casual and multiplayer play, especially Commander, and cares less about win rates than about whether cards and mechanics make for satisfying games.

Neither can catch every combination players will discover. The ongoing banned-and-restricted process is the evidence that Magic's design has to keep adapting after publication.

## The rules team: Magic's systems architects

In September 2026, Wizards started a dedicated _Magic Rules Blog_ and introduced the people who maintain the game's rules infrastructure.

- Jess Dunks, Magic rules architect, leads the long-term architecture of the Comprehensive Rules and works with other teams so new mechanics can coexist with the existing system.
- Eric Levine, rules manager, works on rules development, official card text, release notes, Oracle updates, and the ongoing interpretation of the rules.
- Eliana Rabinowitz, rules specialist, works on mechanics and rules interactions.

Beyond refereeing disputes, the rules team helps decide whether a proposed mechanic can be expressed in Magic's existing model at all. It writes and revises the Comprehensive Rules, maintains Oracle wording, resolves ambiguities, and works with designers before a card is final.

The Game Experience Design team works closely with them: Del Laugel, who leads it, Sam Phelan, Paul Speciel, Matt Tabak, Michael Zhang, and Hans Ziegler. Their job is card text that reads clearly and works precisely.

A designer's intended behavior, a card's printed wording, its Oracle text, and the Comprehensive Rules are four separate layers of one system, and keeping them distinct is fundamental. So is keeping these rules specialists distinct from the people who set tournament policy and ban cards. The jobs are related but not the same.

Anyone who has worked in software will recognize the shape. The Comprehensive Rules are a language specification, and cards are programs written in it. Oracle text is the authoritative statement of what a card does, and templating is its consistent syntax. Rules specialists maintain compatibility as features are added, play designers test emergent behavior, and the banned-and-restricted process patches the failures that get past testing.

Backward compatibility matters because Magic has to absorb decades of existing cards as well as the current release. The hard part is extending the system without breaking the principles that hold it together.

## Worldbuilding, story, and art

Magic's Creative team does far more than add flavor text after the design is done. It includes worldbuilders, narrative designers, story leads, creative directors, art directors, concept artists, editors, and producers. These are the people credited on the 2026 set _Reality Fracture_:

| Person         | Responsibility            |
| -------------- | ------------------------- |
| Doug Beyer     | Worldbuilding vision lead |
| Emily Teng     | Worldbuilding design      |
| Meris Mullaley | Worldbuilding design      |
| Roy Graham     | Story lead                |
| Zack Stella    | Lead art director         |

This team builds the fictional world the cards represent, side by side with the mechanical designers. Worldbuilders decide what creatures, cultures, landscapes, and magic exist in a setting; set designers make sure those elements support coherent play. Sometimes the mechanics ask something of the fiction, and sometimes the fiction sparks a mechanic.

Rosewater described this back-and-forth in October 2026, with one telling detail: worldbuilding is deliberately scoped to produce a little more than the set needs, not a complete fictional civilization. Magic creates the impression of a larger world without necessarily building one.

### Art direction

Designers don't illustrate the thousands of cards Magic produces. Wizards commissions professional artists, often freelancers, through art directors who handle assignments, feedback, and visual consistency. Narrative work is a separate job again. Reality Fracture's credits name, among others, Lauren Bond in creative game design, Kat Morgan as creative manager, Stephanie Cheung in art direction, and Alicia Mickes as senior creative director.

That is how a set with dozens of illustrators and writers can still feel like one world. The consistency comes from art direction.

## The Council of Colors

One of Magic's more distinctive institutions is the **Council of Colors**, founded in 2015 to protect the conceptual and mechanical integrity of the five colors and of colorless cards. Its members review sets for misassigned colors, shifts in established strengths and weaknesses, unintended overlap between colors, long-term damage to the color pie, and departures from each color's philosophy.

Rosewater and Mark Gottlieb oversee the council, with a counselor assigned to each color. As of August 2024, the counselors were:

| Color     | Counselor    |
| --------- | ------------ |
| White     | Corey Bowen  |
| Blue      | Megan Smith  |
| Black     | Chris Mooney |
| Red       | Ben Weitz    |
| Green     | Adam Prosak  |
| Colorless | Daniel Xu    |

The council exists to stop designers, each working on their own set, from slowly eroding the system that lets all those sets work together. The color pie defines at once each color's mechanical capabilities, philosophical identity, strategic strengths, strategic weaknesses, and the constraints on future design. The constraints are the point. Without them, every set could stretch what each color does until the distinctions meant nothing, and in a game where a card from 1998 can still meet one from 2026, that would be ruinous.

## Product engineering and production

Some people who work on Magic never touch a mechanic or a piece of art. The tabletop studio includes product architects, design economists, producers, production engineers, quality-assurance staff, and specialists who coordinate printing and distribution. Two of them explained their jobs in Wizards' 2023 discussion of Play Boosters. Mike Turian, a former professional player, described product architecture as a combination of executive producer and product designer. Chris D'Andrea, a design economist, described working with product engineers to make sure the boosters that get printed match the design.

### Why as-fan matters here

As-fan is more than a design number. It drives printing sheets, rarity distributions, booster collation, Limited play, how visible a mechanic is, and how consistently archetypes come together. If a designer wants a mechanic to show up 1.5 times per booster on average, Set Design works out the distribution of cards and rarities, and the product and production specialists make sure the physical boosters deliver it.

The product is part of the game system. Industrial production is wired directly into the experience of play, and the rules, the math, the printing, and the distribution can't be designed in isolation from one another.

## Who governs organized play?

Making a game and maintaining its competitive life are different jobs. Play Design keeps shaping Magic after cards ship, through format monitoring and ban recommendations. The October 12, 2026, banned-and-restricted announcement, for example, had several authors, among them Carmen Klomparens, Jadine Klomparens, Arya Karamchandani, Eric Engelhard, and Gavin Verhey, each writing about different formats.

### Commander governance

Commander has its own history. In September 2024, the independent Commander Rules Committee handed management of the format to Wizards, which then set up a Commander Format Panel with community representatives. Gavin Verhey is the most visible figure in this work.

### Competitive governance

Tournament policy, judge administration, organized-play operations, and the Pro Tour sit outside core card development. Historically much of organized play ran through the DCI; today it is spread across tournament operations, policy, competitive structures, and format management.

The people who define how Magic works are often different from the ones who decide how it should be played competitively. The rules of the game, the rules of a tournament, and the rules of a format are separate layers of governance.

## How the organization evolved

| Period                               | Organizational character                                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tempest / Urza / Masques, 1997–2000  | Smaller R&D organization, with a strong Design-versus-Development distinction                                  |
| Invasion through Mirrodin, 2000–2004 | Increasingly formal mechanical themes, development responsibilities, and oversight                             |
| 2004–2016                            | Separate Design and Development teams, with growing development and creative staffs                            |
| 2017–2019                            | Reorganization into Vision Design, Set Design, and Play Design, followed by a tabletop studio model            |
| 2024–2026                            | A highly specialized organization addressing competitive, casual, crossover, and physical-product requirements |

Wizards formalized the modern design pipeline in 2017 and moved to its studio structure in 2018–2019.

Rosewater's July 2026 retrospective shows how the lessons were learned. He calls _Urza's Saga_ "historically broken" and the _Mercadian Masques_ block, its response, "watered down"; afterward the organization built stronger processes for thematic planning, balance, and long-term consistency. The early game was made by a small group learning through invention, experiment, and mistakes. Today's game relies on specialized teams and increasingly formal review.

The change cuts both ways. Institutional knowledge makes known mistakes less likely to recur, specialization builds deep expertise, and testing and review improve consistency. But specialization brings coordination costs: more teams must agree before a decision is final, and the distance from an original idea to its published form grows. A process that protects a system from catastrophe can also discourage the unusual experiment.

## Commercial direction and creative authority

Not every major decision about Magic starts in R&D. An arc-planning team sets the sequence of future sets. The Creative team proposes worlds, market research tests ideas, and franchise and product leaders shape the portfolio.

In an October 2026 article, Rosewater said planning discussions were already considering sets for 2031 and 2032. He also described longer lead times for Universes Beyond products, partly because the outside owners of the intellectual property must approve the work.

So the growing number of outside franchises in Magic is an organizational and commercial direction with its own planning and production demands, and the designers then have to make it work as Magic. That matters when judging where the game is going: a set can be brilliantly executed inside a strategy whose merits are open to debate.

Responsibility splits several ways. Corporate leadership sets commercial objectives and allocates resources. Franchise leadership sets longer-term product and brand direction. Game design leadership turns those objectives into playable experiences. Creative leadership gives products a coherent fictional and visual identity. Rules and play design protect the system's mechanical integrity. The roles overlap but aren't interchangeable, so be careful about pinning a commercial decision on a designer just because that designer explains the product in public.

## Where the internal workings are most visible

Wizards has published a good deal of primary material on how Magic is made. These are the most useful for studying Magic as a designed system.

- [Reality Fracture Vision Design Handoff, Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/reality-fracture-vision-design-handoff-part-1): a real internal design handoff, with goals, early mechanical thinking, and the people involved. It shows the gap between initial design intent and the finished product.
- [Playtesting](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/playtesting): Rosewater's August 2026 account of how experimental decks, Draft tests, the Future Future League, and Commander testing work, and how Wizards tries to judge emergent gameplay before release.
- [Magic Rules Blog: Introducing the Blog](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/magic-rules-blog-introducing-the-blog): a direct introduction to the team responsible for rules architecture and the Comprehensive Rules, and a look at the formal machinery behind Magic's increasingly complex interactions.
- [Stages of Design](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/stages-of-design): a history of how Magic design evolved, with an extensive record of contributors, and the best source for comparing today's organization with the one that made earlier sets.

## What this reveals about Magic

What stands out about modern Magic is how thoroughly Wizards has turned thirty years of experience into specialized disciplines, review processes, and institutional knowledge.

The organization has to keep solving several problems at once:

- Invention: new mechanics, worlds, combinations, and experiences.
- Coherence: keeping those inventions recognizably Magic.
- Balance: stopping any card or strategy from overwhelming the environment.
- Compatibility: preserving interactions with decades of existing cards.
- Comprehensibility: letting people understand and play an ever more complex system.
- Production: turning abstract designs into reliable physical products.
- Governance: maintaining formats, organized play, and rules after publication.
- Commerce: sustaining a business that can fund all of it.

These goals collide. A mechanically inventive card can be hard to understand. A compelling fictional idea may not fit the color pie. A card that plays beautifully on its own can become oppressive in a competitive metagame. A commercially attractive product can force creative decisions no one would otherwise make. A stricter approval process can prevent disasters and discourage experiments in the same stroke. These tensions can't be eliminated, only managed.

Magic survives by producing unfamiliar possibilities inside a familiar rules framework. Its organization has to preserve what makes the game recognizable while leaving designers room to find things nobody imagined when Richard Garfield created it. And because Magic is also a commercial product, those creative and technical judgments sit beside decisions about growth, release frequency, and licensing.

To understand the people making Magic is to understand three forces at work at once: invention, institutional stewardship, and commercial direction. The open question isn't whether Wizards can keep inventing cards. It's whether an ever more elaborate institution can keep the imaginative freedom that made those cards worth inventing in the first place.
