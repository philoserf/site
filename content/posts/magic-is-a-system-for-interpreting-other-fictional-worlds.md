---
title: Magic is a system for interpreting other fictional worlds
description: Magic's Star Trek cards have a rule that counts your creature types and leaves the Borg out. A picture of Captain Kirk would be licensing. Is a rule like that something more?
date: 2027-04-09
---

Magic's Star Trek cards have a rule called federation. It counts the different creature types you control and leaves the Borg out of the count. A card with Captain Kirk's picture on it would be licensing. A rule that makes you weigh the Federation's diversity against the Borg's sameness says something about Star Trek, and it says it in Magic's own terms.

That is the strongest case for Universes Beyond. The usual arguments are business arguments: it brings new customers to Magic, makes Wizards more money, and draws younger players to the game. They do little for someone who has spent decades playing Magic and values its identity. The better argument starts from what Magic is. At bottom, Magic: The Gathering is a system for making fictional universes playable, and only secondarily a fictional universe of its own.

Consider what Richard Garfield actually invented. Magic began as a game in which players became powerful wizards, drawing spells, creatures, and artifacts from an imagined collection of possibilities. The cards suggested a larger reality without having to describe it, and players supplied much of the imagination themselves. Early Magic made this plain. Its world-building was fragmentary: mysterious names, evocative illustrations, scraps of flavor text, and an enormous imaginative space between them. The game invited you to make meaning through play.

Magic's first expansion, Arabian Nights, released in December 1993, took its setting and characters straight from an existing body of stories. Garfield chose an established fictional world rather than inventing another one. Modern licensing is a different arrangement, and the objections below say so; still, Arabian Nights settles a historical point: Magic's original identity never depended on keeping its fictional sources inside a proprietary universe. The imaginative premise was always larger than Dominaria.

## The color pie as a vocabulary

White, blue, black, red, and green are more than mechanical categories. They form a powerful vocabulary for values, motivations, institutions, conflicts, and ways of exercising power. Apply that vocabulary to an established fictional world and you get a reading of that world.

Tolkien's story is full of power, sacrifice, corruption, loyalty, temptation, and rival ideas of legitimate authority, tensions Magic has been expressing mechanically for thirty years. The designers of The Lord of the Rings: Tales of Middle-earth had to turn the Ring's temptation into gameplay, and they made a mechanic that keeps temptation running through the player's decisions. It is an imperfect rendering of Tolkien and a real attempt to express literary meaning through game rules, and that is a creative undertaking in its own right.

Final Fantasy went further. Its designers built Saga creatures to stand for summoned beings that arrive, act over several turns, and depart, so Magic's existing mechanics became a way of reading another game's story and combat. Star Trek goes further still: with federation, the difference between cooperation through diversity and assimilation into enforced sameness becomes a deckbuilding decision.

## A different creative constraint

There is another argument, especially for someone who remembers Magic before its setting was so thoroughly built out. Magic was once far more mysterious than it is now. Over the decades its fiction has gathered histories, cosmologies, institutions, recurring characters, and narrative obligations. That is a real achievement, and it is also a set of constraints. A return to Dominaria must reckon with Dominaria's history, a return to Ravnica must make room for its guilds, and a new story about familiar planeswalkers must square with what players already know of them.

Universes Beyond poses a different problem. Instead of asking how to extend Magic's own fiction, designers ask how Magic's mechanical vocabulary can express someone else's imaginative system. That kind of creativity is especially productive, because the designers don't control the source material and have to find correspondences between two systems that already exist. Constraints often make for good design.

## Two ways into the same table

The social argument deserves more respect than it usually gets. Two longtime players can spend an afternoon arguing about whether a card should be blue or white, because years of play have taught them the philosophy behind the colors. A new player who loves Star Trek can join that conversation from the other side. They already understand the Borg, the Federation, and the philosophies those institutions stand for, and Magic hands them a new language for something familiar. The longtime player and the Star Trek fan now share a game, and neither has to give up what they already love.

The exchange runs both ways. The Star Trek fan learns the color pie, and the Magic player meets another fictional system through familiar mechanics. Each is making connections across two worlds, and that is how games build cultures.

## The objections stand

None of this dissolves the legitimate objections. Arabian Nights and a Spider-Man card are different things. Public-domain mythology belongs to a shared cultural inheritance, while a modern licensed character belongs to a commercial rights holder, and the two arrangements mean different things for creative freedom, availability, and the long-term stewardship of the game. There is also a real aesthetic problem when incompatible worlds share a battlefield. A player who loves Magic's own fantasy atmosphere can reasonably find superheroes, space marines, and cartoon turtles jarring.

That problem has become harder to avoid. Starting with Final Fantasy in 2025, full Universes Beyond sets became legal in Standard. The 2026 schedule held four licensed sets and three in-universe ones, and Wizards has announced three of each for 2027. That changes the experience of Constructed Magic itself, beyond the range of optional products on the shelf. Commercial and creative incentives don't always align, and there's no point pretending they do.

But those concerns bear on how Universes Beyond should be done, not on whether it should exist. A badly conceived crossover reduces Magic to a licensing platform; a well-conceived one widens what the game can say. The difference shows at the table. Does the design produce interesting decisions? Does it make familiar mechanics behave in unexpected ways? Does it reward knowing the source without requiring it? Would the card still be interesting if you'd never heard of the property? An experienced Magic player is unusually well equipped to answer those questions.

## A language grows by what it can say

For more than thirty years Magic has shown that a small set of foundational rules can generate an extraordinary range of experiences. The color pie, the mana system, combat, deck construction, and the interactions between cards together work like a language, and a language grows richer through what people find ways to say with it.

So the interesting question is not whether Gandalf, Captain Kirk, or a Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtle belongs in Magic's fictional universe. In the ordinary narrative sense, they don't. The interesting question is whether Magic can tell us something about those characters and their worlds that their original media cannot. If it can, Universes Beyond shows something remarkable about Garfield's invention: Magic has become a creative medium sophisticated enough to interpret other fictional worlds through its own rules. For someone who has played since the early years, that possibility deserves exploration before dismissal.
