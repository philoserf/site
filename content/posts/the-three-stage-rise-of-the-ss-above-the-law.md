---
title: The three-stage rise of the SS above the law
description: Traces how the SS moved from extralegal party militia to a self-governing state within the state, and finally to abolishing legal order entirely, with each step requiring tacit permission from those in power.
date: 2026-10-21
---

The story of the SS can be told in three steps. It began outside the law, rose above it, and ended by undoing it. Told that briefly, the story shows what longer accounts tend to leave out: each step needed the permission of people who still had the power to refuse. The sequence looks inevitable now, but it was not. It was ratified three times.

Beginning outside the law is, by itself, unremarkable. Gangs, militias and revolutionary cells all work outside the legal order without threatening it. The early SS was a party bodyguard unit, its violence something the movement's leaders could disavow while profiting from it. The relationship was parasitic and, to the state, manageable. The bargain in this stage is what matters: legitimate institutions treating extralegal violence as a tool rather than a threat, so long as it served their short-term interests.

The move from outside the law to above it is the moment the parasite becomes the host. To stand above the law is to claim that the organization's purposes outrank the legal order and need not answer to it. Under Himmler the SS won that standing. It ran its own courts, its own businesses, its own intelligence service, and claimed a racial mission that mere legality could not contain; historians call it a state within the state, and Eugen Kogon titled his study of it simply _Der SS-Staat_ (_The SS State_). This is not lawlessness. It is a rival claim to legitimacy, and it is more dangerous than crime because it gives obedience a moral vocabulary. Men rarely commit atrocities believing they are breaking rules. They commit them believing they serve a higher order.

Undoing the law goes past even this: it dissolves the idea that any legal order could bind power. By the middle of the war the SS had dissolved it within its domain. In the camps and the occupied East no law applied, no appeal existed, and nothing stood between the organization's will and its execution.

The pattern is not peculiar to the SS or to fascism. Tolerated illegality, then claimed superiority, then the end of legal order: it recurs wherever those in power treat checks as obstacles instead of foundations. No organization undoes the law it has not first been allowed to stand outside, and none rises above the law without the consent of those still nominally administering it. Read the three stages in order and you hear three doors close. Each one had someone standing beside it.
