---
title: Why government is not weather
description: "Critique of the maxim to treat government like weather: weather has no author while government is made of contestable decisions. Accepts the Stoic reading as personal discipline but rejects it as a theory of citizenship, since it produces the powerlessness it describes."
date: 2026-11-02
aliases:
  - Government Is Not Weather
---

Treat government like weather, the advice goes: not something to control, but something to adapt to. It recommends a posture, accommodation over agency, and its appeal is immediate. Most people, most of the time, experience government roughly as they experience a cold front, a large impersonal force that arrives on its own schedule, indifferent to their preferences, best met with the civic equivalent of a coat. Against the exhausting modern expectation that one should have opinions about everything and be perpetually mobilized, the counsel to adapt offers relief.

But the metaphor works beneath the relief, and it works on the one point where weather and government differ. Weather has no author. No one decided this morning's temperature; it is the output of physics, answerable to no will and open to no appeal. Government is the opposite kind of thing. It is made of decisions, staffed by people, and sustained by the participation, or the acquiescence, of the governed. To file it under weather is to treat a set of choices as a climate.

That reframing serves whoever holds power, because it recasts their decisions as conditions. A tax policy, a war, a pattern of enforcement: each becomes a season to dress for rather than a choice to contest. The party that profits most from being thought of as weather is the one that would otherwise have to answer for being a person, and regimes that want docile subjects encourage the perception that the way things are is the way things are.

There is a more defensible reading. For any single person, most of government is beyond control. One vote does not move a budget; one citizen cannot repeal a law by conviction. Epictetus opened the Enchiridion by dividing what is up to us from what is not, and the division is sound psychology here: raging at the immovable corrodes the rager and changes nothing. Read narrowly, the advice says only this: do not stake your peace on outcomes you cannot individually determine, and build a life robust across political conditions rather than hostage to them. That is wisdom, not fatalism.

The trouble is that the metaphor cannot stay narrow. It collapses a distinction the Stoics kept: weather is beyond everyone's control, while government is beyond any one person's control and well within reach of people acting together. Adaptation is the right individual answer to a hurricane and an incomplete one to a policy, because a policy has a second address, the collective, at which it can be answered. Counsel that treats the two as identical does not merely describe powerlessness. It generalizes a private limitation into a public principle, and so helps produce the condition it claims only to observe. If enough people decide government is weather, it becomes weather: unaccountable because unattended.

So the advice is a half-truth, dangerous in proportion to how completely it is believed. As a personal discipline it is humane and necessary. As a theory of citizenship it is abdication dressed as serenity. The harder stance is to adapt to government as weather in the part of your life that is yours alone, and to refuse in the part you share with others, where the front moving in has an author, and authors can be made to answer. The most effective way to remove something from human control is to persuade everyone it was never under human control to begin with.
