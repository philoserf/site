---
title: Will Congress restore its power after the midterms
description: After Watergate, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution and the Budget Act, and both eroded. A new majority promises oversight. Would its limits hold when the president is its own?
date: 2026-11-24
---

Written 2026-11-04, the day after the midterms.

**Some recovery is likely; a lasting restoration is not, at roughly 1 in 5 by my estimate.** These are judgment calls, not modeled forecasts, and they turn on what "restore" means:

| Outcome by about 2030                                                | Rough odds                                       |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| More oversight, subpoenas and hearings                               | Very likely, about 90%, given the new majorities |
| Regular appropriations bills passed on time more often               | About 30–40%                                     |
| Congress takes back real power over war, tariffs or spending, in law | About 20–25%                                     |
| That power holds even when the president is from the majority party  | About 10–15%                                     |

**The last row is the real test.** Congress tends to give up power to presidents of its own party, because party loyalty usually beats institutional loyalty. After Watergate, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution and the Budget Act, and both eroded over the following decades. Any law limiting the president before 2029 would also need two-thirds of both chambers to survive a veto, and these majorities don't have it. So the moment that counts may be 2029: if a Democrat wins, this Congress's successors will be asked to restrain their own president.

## Do centrist candidates follow from the midterms?

The result doesn't settle it. The president's party usually loses ground in midterms, so wins like these are partly a verdict on the party in power. Before crediting moderation, compare how moderate and populist candidates ran against each district's baseline. Richard Ojeda's win over Richard Hudson in North Carolina's 9th District, for one, doesn't obviously read as centrist.

Moderation has some support. Research has found that more ideological nominees tend to do worse in general elections, though later work suggests the penalty has shrunk as voters have grown more partisan. The evidence is also weaker for presidential races, where the electorate and the stakes differ. Others argue that turnout and an anti-establishment message matter more than ideology, and that debate isn't settled.

Winning and restoring are different problems. A centrist president may win more easily, but that doesn't make Congress stronger. A popular president of any ideology gets deference, and centrists have no special record of giving power back.

## What would move the odds

More than any nominee's ideology, what matters is whether candidates commit to specific changes before they hold power: automatic expiry of emergency powers, limits on tariff delegation, stronger impoundment enforcement, and rules that let rank-and-file members force votes. If those go into the platform and pass while the party controls Congress, the bottom row rises noticeably.
