---
title: Infinity as fiction and the imaginary as real
description: A theory of everything will become clear, the claim goes, once we drop the fiction of infinity and grant the imaginary its reality. Half of that is shrewd. Which half?
date: 2027-01-11
---

One claim runs: a theory of everything will become clear once we remove the fiction of infinity and embrace the reality of the imaginary component of complex numbers in domains that haven't completed the transformation. It is best understood as a metaphysical proposal wearing the vocabulary of physics, one that identifies two genuine sore points in foundational science and proposes that resolving our attitude toward them is what stands between us and unification. That diagnosis is partly shrewd and partly a category error, and the two halves are worth separating.

On infinity, the statement keeps respectable company. Infinities are the recurring symptom of a physical theory in distress: the ultraviolet divergences that forced renormalization onto quantum field theory, a procedure its own architects found suspect; the singularities at the center of black holes and at the Big Bang, which most physicists read as the point where a theory announces its own breakdown. A finite final theory is a defensible expectation. But calling infinity a fiction overreaches. Physical infinity (a divergent quantity, an unbounded curvature) is a different object from mathematical infinity, the actual infinite of the continuum. A theory can be physically finite while still built on infinite mathematical structures, and the complex numbers the statement wants to keep are themselves an infinite, continuous field. You cannot discard infinity wholesale and retain the reality of the imaginary unit; the imaginary axis is a feature of a continuum.

The second half is stronger than it first appears. For decades the i in the Schrödinger equation was treated as bookkeeping, a convenience that could in principle be unwound into pairs of real numbers. That assumption proved fragile: a 2021 experiment came down against a strictly real-valued quantum mechanics. Later theoretical work disputes whether that verdict concerns real numbers as such or only the composition rule the test assumed. Complex numbers are not optional furniture in quantum theory; nature appears to insist on them. So embracing the reality of the imaginary component points at something true. The slippage is in the word reality. That complex amplitudes are indispensable to the formalism does not make the imaginary part a place, a hidden region one might visit. It is a degree of freedom in a state. The statement reifies the imaginary—treats it geographically, as a domain—and that is a leap well past what indispensability licenses.

"Domains that haven't completed the transformation" is the most obscure phrase and the most evocative, and it is where the proposal stops being physics and becomes something closer to process philosophy. Physics does contain rotations between the real and the imaginary: Wick rotation exchanges ordinary time for imaginary time, converting a causal, Lorentzian world into a statistical, Euclidean one, and imaginary time has been put to serious use in quantum cosmology. The image of a domain caught mid-rotation, partly actual and partly imaginary, has a faint formal echo there. But the deeper reading is older. The imaginary number is mathematics' emblem of rotation that has not yet become position, of phase rather than magnitude, of becoming rather than being. To grant it reality is to grant ontological standing to the unfinished. The name "imaginary" was originally a slur. Descartes meant it dismissively, and the history of mathematics is largely the history of such slurs being retracted, as negative and irrational numbers were each admitted late and grudgingly. The intuition underneath the statement is that what we call imaginary may be what the not-yet-completed looks like from inside a world that mistakes stasis for the only form of existence.

What the statement gets wrong is "will become clear." That is a promissory note with nothing behind it. The obstacles to unification (quantizing gravity, the measurement problem, the hierarchy of scales) are not dissolved by deciding that infinity is fiction and the imaginary is real; they are problems of mechanism and prediction, and relabeling which concepts count as real supplies neither. The proposal has the architecture of a keystone insight, the single conceptual correction that drops everything else into place, and the history of physics rarely rewards that hope; relativity and quantum mechanics were revolutions of formalism and tested consequence.

Yet the chiasmus at its center is worth keeping, because it generalizes far past physics. We have called real a thing that may be fiction, and called imaginary a thing that may be real. Understanding advances by exactly that double move—the dismissal of the assumed and the reclamation of the dismissed—and the pull toward treating the completed and the static as more legitimate than the rotational and in-progress is a habit of mind, not a finding about the world. The statement's lasting value is not as a route to a theory of everything but as a caution: the line between what we file as fictional and what we file as real is drawn by us, early, and often in the wrong place.
