---
title: Why the dissolve-the-union argument fails
description: Critique of the claim that unresolved political differences justify dissolving the United States, examining its assumptions about resolvable disagreement, historical urgency, a unified "we," and the practical incoherence of partition given interleaved partisans.
date: 2026-09-16
---

Threats of dissolution have a long American pedigree, but the formulation that "if we do not resolve our political differences soon it will become time to dissolve these dis-United States" deserves scrutiny because its tidy wordplay disguises a stack of contestable assumptions. The first is that political differences are the sort of thing that gets resolved. Policy disputes get adjudicated, coalitions reshuffle, electoral majorities shift, but the underlying facts that political life exists to manage—scarcity, value pluralism, irreducibly competing visions of the good—remain unresolved on any human timescale. A free polity is the institution that holds disagreement productively without needing it to disappear. To demand resolution as the price of continued union is to misunderstand what a union is for.

The temporal urgency is no sturdier. The American republic has weathered the Alien and Sedition Acts, nullification, an actual civil war, Reconstruction's violent collapse, the Gilded Age's labor wars, the Palmer Raids, McCarthyism, and the long convulsions of the 1960s and 70s, every one of them producing commentators certain that dissolution was imminent or warranted. That the diagnosis recurs without the predicted outcome makes it likelier that present polarization, whatever its real costs, belongs to a familiar genre than that it is an unprecedented exception. Calling the moment terminal is rhetorically gratifying but epistemically cheap; it costs the speaker nothing and absolves them of the slower work of coalition-building, persuasion, and institutional repair.

Everything rests on the pronoun. "If we do not resolve" implies a unified subject capable of collective decision—the very thing the statement claims is in jeopardy. There is no "we" that sits above the differences and could choose to resolve or dissolve. There are only factions, each of which experiences the other as the obstacle to coherence, each of which would describe its preferred resolution as healing and the other's as capitulation. The grammatical fiction of a deliberating collective body makes the threat sound reasonable; strip the pronoun and what remains is a faction announcing it will leave if it does not get its way, which is the structure of every secessionist appeal on record, and rarely one history judges sympathetically.

The dissolution itself, considered concretely, dissolves into incoherence. The 1861 model failed, at a cost of three-quarters of a million lives. The Czechoslovak velvet divorce worked because the populations were geographically discrete and the stakes were small. American partisans are interleaved at every scale—red counties inside blue states, blue cities inside red states, ideologically mixed households inside both. Any serious partition would require either population transfer on a scale unseen since the subcontinent in 1947 or a map of gerrymandered enclaves so absurd that the resulting polities would inherit every governance pathology of the original plus new ones. The Yugoslav case is the more honest analogue, and it is not an argument for trying.

The most generous reading is that the sentence makes a diagnosis rather than issuing an ultimatum—that something has already broken and saying so plainly is the only honest response. Even granting this, what follows dissolution still has to be answered, and the historical answer is rarely that the separated parts achieve the harmony their separation was supposed to produce. Smaller polities discover that they too contain factions; the dynamic that drove the first split reproduces inside each fragment, since the conflict was never about borders. The harder and less satisfying conclusion is that the differences are the country, and that learning to lose elections without seeking exit is the democratic discipline. Resolution and dissolution are not two options. The rhyme is the whole of the resemblance.
