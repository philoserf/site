---
title: Creation myths as encoded cosmology
description: "Creation myths share a logical constraint with cosmology, not scientific foresight: every origin starts undifferentiated. Physics keeps the shape and reverses the sign—the formless beginning was the most ordered state of all."
date: 2026-12-04
---

Genesis, the Enuma Elish, the Nasadiya Sukta, and the Popol Vuh are usually read one of two ways: as primitive science that got the world wrong, or as scripture that secretly got it right. Both readings treat a creation myth as a report of something seen. It reads better as the answer to a problem, and the problem has not changed.

The second reading is the tempting one. Genesis opens with darkness and then light, and the early universe has a moment when light breaks free: recombination, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when the cooling plasma became neutral gas and the universe turned transparent. The match fails on inspection. Before recombination the universe was full of light, only opaque; after it, that glow cooled into the infrared and a long darkness followed before the first stars. The physical order is light, then dark, then stars. Genesis has it the other way around. Press any of these parallels and it fails the same way. No Iron Age poet saw the first moments of the universe, and a reading that needs one to have seen them collapses as soon as you check the timeline.

What the myths share with cosmology is not an observation but a constraint. Any account of an origin has to begin with a state that holds none of the distinctions it later explains. If the first state already had sky and earth, light and dark, this and that, nothing would have been created; it would only have been rearranged. So the beginning must be undifferentiated, and creation must be the arrival of difference. That is logic before it is physics, and anyone who reasons carefully about origins runs into it.

Every myth here meets it. Genesis begins with a formless earth and dark waters, and its first acts are divisions: light from darkness, the waters above from the waters below, sea from dry land. In the Enuma Elish, Marduk splits the body of Tiamat, the salt sea, in two and makes one half the sky. Hesiod's Chaos is a gap, and the Norse Ginnungagap a yawning one. Egypt's Nun is a dark, inert water from which the first mound rises. Even creation by speech fits, because to name a thing is to set it apart from everything it is not. God says "Let there be light," and the makers in the Popol Vuh say "Earth," and the earth rises like a mist.

The obvious objection is the craftsman. Plenty of myths have a maker who shapes the world from materials: the earth-diver who brings mud up from the floor of the first sea, the Norse gods building the world from the body of the giant Ymir. But look at where the craftsman starts. The earth-diver dives into undifferentiated water. Ymir himself is born in the void, where the ice of Niflheim meets the heat of Muspelheim. The making comes second; the formless state comes first. Craft is one more way to tell differentiation, and it never escapes the constraint.

Cosmology faces the same constraint, and there it can be tested. The standard account fits it easily: an early universe hot and nearly uniform, its forces not yet distinct, and the structure we live in traced to symmetry breaking and to slight unevenness growing under gravity. The harder test comes from proposals that remove the beginning. Hartle and Hawking's no-boundary proposal has no first moment; asking what came before the Big Bang is, in an image Hawking liked, asking what lies south of the South Pole. Yet the state it starts from is as plain as the geometry allows, nearly smooth, its fluctuations at their lowest. Penrose's conformal cyclic cosmology, a minority view, has no beginning either. Each age of the universe ends when its matter is gone and only radiation remains, and radiation keeps no clock and knows no scale; that featureless end is, in the mathematics, the next age's beginning. Take away the beginning and the undifferentiated state is still there, at every seam. The constraint is about where explanation stops, whether or not there is a first moment.

Physics reverses one thing no myth could have reasoned its way to. The myths treat the formless state—the deep, the chaos, the sea monster Marduk has to subdue—as disorder, and creation as order imposed on it. Under gravity the accounting flips. Matter spread smooth and even is the least likely arrangement there is, its entropy as low as it gets, and its clumping into stars and galaxies is entropy rising. Penrose put the odds of so smooth a beginning at one in a number whose zeros outnumber the particles in the observable universe. The formless state the myths feared as chaos is the most ordered the universe has ever been, and everything since, ourselves included, is its undoing. The myths had the shape right and the sign wrong, and the sign was out of their reach: every disorder they ever watched, smoke spreading and water settling, ran toward the even and the smooth. Only gravity, at the scale of galaxies, runs the other way.

The Nasadiya Sukta is the one text that states the constraint outright. "There was neither non-existence nor existence then," it begins: the first state cannot be described in the categories it precedes, not even being and not-being. Then it does what most popular accounts of the Big Bang do not. It admits it cannot see past the beginning, and ends by wondering whether even the one who looks down from the highest heaven knows. Cosmology has not settled the question of initial conditions either. The hymn simply says so.

None of this makes a creation myth true as a report of events. The world was not spoken into being by a deity. These stories carry the structure of the problem, worked out by people reasoning honestly about where things come from, in the only vocabulary they had. The constraint survived the encoding. Where the stories go wrong, the loss happened in the codec or the evidence, never in the reasoning.
