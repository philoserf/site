---
title: The feeling of arriving too late for discovery
description: Explores the recurring historical illusion that all major discoveries and frontiers are exhausted, from Michelson's 1894 claim before quantum theory and relativity to Fukuyama's "end of history," arguing the feeling reflects a wish to be a protagonist rather than an inheritor.
date: 2026-12-25
---

In July 1894, dedicating the Ryerson Physical Laboratory at the University of Chicago, Albert Michelson told his audience that "the more important fundamental laws and facts of physical science have all been discovered." What remained was refinement: "Our future discoveries must be looked for in the sixth place of decimals." Six years later Max Planck introduced the quantum. Eleven years later Einstein published special relativity, and Michelson's own 1887 experiment with Edward Morley became part of its evidence.

Michelson wasn't a fool. He was voicing, with confidence, what most people feel as melancholy: arriving after everything has been done, with no bright unknown horizons left—the sense that history's meaningful chapters closed before you entered them. The feeling is easy to share. Every map looks finished, every peak climbed, every ideological war fought. None of that is quite true. Seabed 2030 counts only about 27 percent of the ocean floor as mapped to modern standards. The feeling doesn't wait for a survey.

And it runs deeper than inventory. It's about where you stand in the story—the difference between protagonist and inheritor. Protagonists face unknowns where their choices matter. Inheritors manage existing systems, optimize known processes, refine rather than discover. One creates meaning; the other finds it pre-packaged. Michelson's sixth place of decimals is the inheritor's job description.

Belatedness breeds nostalgia for eras you never lived and a suspicion that maintenance, however competent, lacks the existential weight of creation. You start to wonder whether you're living as a footnote to someone else's achievement.

Michelson had company. Livy, writing his history of Rome as Augustus took power, opened it by mourning an age "when we can endure neither our vices nor their cure"—at the start of two centuries of the Pax Romana. In 1989 Francis Fukuyama asked in _The National Interest_ whether liberal democracy marked "the end point of mankind's ideological evolution." The end of history keeps arriving, then revealing itself as intermission. The map redraws itself.

The feeling persists anyway, because objective frontiers were never the point. You want to be a protagonist, not an inheritor, and from inside the present you can't tell which you are. Michelson couldn't, and the evidence was his own experiment.
