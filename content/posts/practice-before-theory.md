---
title: Practice Before Theory
description: Throughout history, practical capability has almost always preceded theoretical understanding, not the other way around.
date: 2026-09-14
---

Throughout human history, we created things that worked before we understood how they worked, and the inversion of that order—understanding first, then capability—is a recent and partial accomplishment. People smelted bronze for two thousand years before anyone could have told you what an alloy was. Bread rose, wine fermented, and cheese aged for millennia before Pasteur named the organisms doing the work. Willow bark eased pain, cinchona broke fevers, and ether silenced the operating theater long before mechanism met substance. Domestication turned wolves into dogs, grasses into wheat, and aurochs into cattle—a process no breeder could have explained in genetic terms. Watt's engine pulled coal out of mines well before Carnot worked out why heat engines have the efficiencies they do; the Wright brothers flew while mathematicians still argued over the equations of lift.

The ordering is not a quaint feature of the prescientific past. We still don't fully understand anesthesia. Aspirin's mechanism wasn't identified until 1971. Lithium continues to stabilize moods for reasons that remain sketchy. The Maillard reaction was named in 1912, but every cook who had ever browned an onion was already exploiting it. What we call know-how is older than what we call knowledge, and for most of history the two were not closely coupled. People learned by doing, taught by demonstrating, and accumulated capability by selecting for what kept working—a process closer to evolution than to deduction.

The asymmetry conceals a deeper point about where understanding actually lives. The received view: real knowledge is propositional and expressible in textbooks; practical skill is just its downstream application. That's a flattering self-portrait of the theoretical class. The history runs the other way. The world teaches through feedback. Tools, recipes, and routines absorb that teaching long before any explicit theory catches up. Theory, when it arrives, often turns out to be a compression—a useful summary of what successful practice already encoded—rather than the foundation that practice was supposedly built on.

The temptation, especially after the scientific revolution, is to read the prescientific record as a long fumbling toward the modern era, as if our ancestors were proto-scientists who hadn't yet gotten the hang of the method. The asymmetry corrects that conceit. They were not failing at our project; they were succeeding at theirs. They were running an enormous, multigenerational process of variation and selection over techniques, and the techniques that survived were the ones the world rewarded. That this produced cathedrals, sailing ships, proto-vaccines, and farmable continents without first producing physics ought to provoke some humility about which way the dependency really runs.

The observation also illuminates the present with uncomfortable clarity. We now build large neural networks that work better than their builders can explain. Researchers screen drugs by methods whose hits regularly outrun mechanistic accounts. Complex software systems behave in ways no member of the team fully grasps. It's easy to read this as regression—we used to understand what we built, now we don't—when in fact it is a return to the historical norm after a brief, atypical interlude in which understanding and capability marched roughly in step. The interlude was real and valuable; nuclear reactors and semiconductors did require theory first. But it was the exception. The condition in which our artifacts outrun our explanations is the one humans have lived in for nearly all of their making.

What this asks of anyone trying to build well is a particular seriousness about practice. Respect the working artifact even when its principles are obscure. Trust accumulated craft and the people who carry it. And recognize that explanation, when it finally comes, is more often the recovery of what was already known by hand than the revelation of something that was never there. Treat theory as something owed to working practice rather than as a license to dismiss it.
