---
title: The republic must justify itself to the people, not the reverse
description: '"We the people" coexisted with slavery and property qualifications for voting. Someone facing insecure housing or arbitrary policing may ask what they are being recruited to save. Which parts of the republic deserve defending?'
date: 2026-12-01
---

Preserving the republic matters only insofar as it preserves, and expands, people's ability to govern their own lives. Its age, symbols, and constitutional language do not entitle it to survive. If "save the republic" means protecting existing arrangements from people demanding something better, the appeal deserves rejection.

The usual defense of the republic has a weakness: it treats institutions as things citizens should defend without asking hard enough whom those institutions have defended. A person facing insecure housing, arbitrary policing, unaffordable care, or work that leaves no room to live has reason to ask what exactly they are being recruited to save.

The founding contradiction ran deep. "We the people" coexisted with slavery, Indigenous dispossession, the political exclusion of women, and property qualifications for voting. Those were arrangements of power, not lapses in an otherwise inclusive system. Later expansions of citizenship and political equality came through struggle against people and institutions that claimed to be safeguarding the established order.

But to say it never served the people erases the achievements of those who forced it to serve them. Abolition, broader suffrage, labor protections, Social Security, and civil rights legislation changed lives materially. Being incomplete does not make them mere talk. Nor does the credit belong only to the constitutional system: movements organized, disrupted, and demanded, and institutions sometimes accommodated them and sometimes resisted them brutally.

The useful distinction is between preserving the existing distribution of power and preserving the means to change it. The two can conflict. A Senate, a court, an electoral arrangement, or an economic institution does not deserve protection simply because it belongs to the republic. It should be judged by how it distributes authority, protects people from domination, and permits correction.

The strongest reason to resist authoritarian consolidation is therefore practical. Concentrated power makes an already unequal society harder to change. When rulers can punish organizing, suppress reporting, disregard votes, and enforce laws selectively, people lose the instruments they need to challenge both government and private power. A government that answers to you poorly is bad; one that can stop you from making it answer is worse.

That argument cannot become an endless instruction to accept deprivation because something worse is possible. Defending political freedoms has to come with a program for distributing real power: whether people can organize at work without retaliation, get justice without wealth, influence public decisions, and live with enough security to take part in civic life. A ballot is indispensable, but it cannot carry the whole meaning of self-government.

So the objective needs revising. We should defend the freedoms and public capacities through which people can build a more democratic order, and stay willing to transform the institutions that obstruct it. Constitutional change, economic reform, and democratic defense belong to the same undertaking.

You owe the republic no gratitude for promises it has failed to keep. The reason to defend any part of it is that doing so helps people gain and use power over their common life. The republic should have to justify itself to the people; the people should not have to justify their demands to the republic.
