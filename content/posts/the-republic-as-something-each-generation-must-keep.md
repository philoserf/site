---
title: The republic as something each generation must keep
date: 2026-07-03
---

A republic has to be kept. Anniversaries resist that idea. They reach for permanence—enduring, robust, exceptional, providential—and a 250th birthday invites the grandest version of the reflex: we have arrived, the founding is secure, the inheritance complete. The truer description runs the other way. The American order is fragile, and it has been kept intact anyway. Continuity is achievement, not destiny. The republic did not have to last. That it has lasted rests on choices made and renewed.

This claim sits in an old tradition. Madison and the other Federalists wrote against the record of failed republics; the founders knew their project might join Athens, Rome, and the Italian city-states in cautionary memory. Franklin's reported remark—"a republic, if you can keep it"—belongs to the same spirit. To say the system has been kept recovers the founders' humility from the later habit of treating survival as effortless. Survival took effort; virtue and geography guaranteed nothing.

To say a republic is kept intact raises an objection. Whether the system held depends on what one takes the system to be. The Civil War broke continuity by any plausible reading. Reconstruction collapsed, and the long Jim Crow era stripped millions of the citizenship the Constitution promised. To call that intact is to identify the system with the formal continuity of Congress, courts, and elections rather than with its substantive promises. The story of unbroken endurance is partly true, and partly an artifact of which scale and which people one counts. The system has been broken and partly repaired more than once, and that repair has itself been preservation.

Better to treat recommitment as the work of each generation. That work refuses the finality anniversaries invite. Founding becomes ongoing rather than a fixed event in 1776 or 1787 or 1865. Arendt argued that a constitution's authority depends on continual reauthorization; Jefferson held that the earth belongs to the living, who owe no automatic debt to the choices of the dead. Each generation inherits institutions it did not build and decides, by its conduct, whether to ratify them again.

Nobody says what that recommitment requires, and the vagueness is not innocent. A call to do the necessary work can mean almost anything. One reader hears a defense of threatened norms against erosion; another, a demand to extend the system's promises to the still-excluded; another, a summons to reform machinery grown rigid or unjust. Broad assent hides the real question, which is not whether the work is necessary but which work is. A sentence everyone endorses says little.

What survives is a disposition toward inherited political life: pride held with vigilance, gratitude with responsibility, confidence with the knowledge that nothing built by human beings is secure against human beings. The disposition reaches well past national politics. Left alone, things come apart; marriages, friendships, trades, scientific traditions, languages last because someone maintains them. A 250-year frame shows at national scale what holds everywhere else: the feat is remarkable because it would fail tomorrow if no one chose to go on.
