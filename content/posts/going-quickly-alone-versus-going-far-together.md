---
title: Going quickly alone versus going far together
description: Explores the proverb about solo speed versus collective endurance, arguing wisdom lies in matching solitude or collaboration to the true distance of the goal rather than treating either as an inherent virtue.
date: 2026-12-23
---

If you want to go quickly, go alone; if you want to go far, go together. The proverb, often attributed to West African oral tradition, draws a line between two kinds of journey and warns that the means suited to one will betray the other. Speed is the property of the unencumbered. A solitary traveler answers to no one's pace, deliberates with no one, carries only their own provisions and their own doubts. Distance is the property of the supported. The companion lifts when the leg fails, remembers the route when memory falters, carries water when one canteen is empty, and supplies the reason to continue when reasons run thin. The proverb claims these two virtues, velocity and endurance, pull against each other: to optimize for one is to sacrifice the other.

The opposition is real but conditional. Coordination has costs: meetings, compromises, the friction of explaining oneself, the tax of accommodating another temperament. A single mind running a single thread will outpace a committee on most short-horizon problems, which is why startups beat incumbents to market and why lone scholars sometimes leap past institutes. But the same factors that make solitary speed possible—no redundancy, no relief, no diversification of risk—are what make solitary distance precarious. A single illness, a single failure of nerve, a single bad decision unchecked by another perspective, and the journey ends. Companions slow the pace by demanding accommodation; they extend the journey by surviving the things that would have ended a soloist.

Read literally, the proverb concerns travel, but its grip on the imagination comes from how cleanly it maps to nearly every sustained human undertaking. Marriages, businesses, and social movements all face the same tension between the propulsion that comes from a unified will and the durability that comes from shared burden. Individuals or small cells moving fast often start revolutions; they survive, when they survive, because they build coalitions that move slowly. Some companies founded by a single visionary outpace their competitors for a decade and then collapse for want of the institutional ballast that would have slowed them down earlier.

A more uncomfortable reading hides in the gentle wording. The choice between going quickly and going far is often imposed. Some people travel alone because no one will go with them; others travel in company because they cannot move without help. Neither chose a virtue. The proverb presents an entrepreneur's choice—pick your virtue—when in many lives the constraint comes first and the virtue is rationalized after. To take the saying seriously is also to ask what kind of company is actually available, what kind of pace one's circumstances allow, and whether the goal is far enough away to justify the slower march.

The saying's deepest point is that wisdom lies in matching means to ends. Solitude and company are tools, each calibrated to a different distance. The error the proverb warns against is not loneliness or sociability but mismatch—sprinting in formation, or attempting a generational project alone. What arrives first is rarely what lasts. A life that wants both has to know, at each fork, which journey it is on.
