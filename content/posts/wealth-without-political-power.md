---
title: Wealth Without Political Power
description: Wealth is only secure when institutions, not personal political leverage, protect property from rulers' claims.
date: 2026-10-16
---

There is a saying that it is dangerous to be rich and not powerful. Read as advice to rich people it is cynical and mostly wrong. Read as a description of a kind of state it is exact. The danger arises wherever political authority is strong enough to threaten property and too weakly constrained to respect it, and that is a defect in the state, not a gap in anyone's personal stock of influence.

History supplies the pattern in quantity. Medieval kings borrowed from Jewish financiers, Italian bankers and rich merchants because taxation could not raise what war cost. The creditors became indispensable while remaining defenseless, a fatal combination. Edward I expelled England's Jews in 1290 after decades of taxing and seizing what they had. Philip IV expelled France's in 1306 and arrested the Templars, to whom he owed money, the next year. Edward III defaulted on the Bardi and Peruzzi in the 1340s and broke them. In each case the danger arose from the wealth itself. A poor outsider might be despised; a rich one owned things worth taking.

The logic runs past confiscation. Wealth attracts claims because it can be turned into anything, including opposition. A rich merchant can fund rivals, buy a press or move his capital abroad. Even where the owner has no political ambition, rulers read an independent fortune as a latent political force, which is why autocracies either absorb wealth into the governing coalition or make holding it conditional on obedience. Russia after Yukos runs the same play: an oligarch holds companies, yachts and billions, and discovers that legal ownership protects nothing once favor lapses. The wealth looks enormous from below and strangely contingent from above.

This exposes what everyday talk about ownership leaves implicit. Property does not defend itself. A deed, a bank balance and a share certificate have force because courts enforce them, administrators respect them, and governments accept limits on their own power to seize. Strip those away and the paper is paper. The secure rich person in a constitutional state is already protected by political power, not their own but the accumulated kind held in independent courts, predictable taxation, a press that can report, and the ordinary citizen's ability to challenge the government. Private wealth stays private because political power is not concentrated in one place.

That is why the saying fails as advice. A society in which every rich person needs direct political leverage is not well ordered; it is a patronage system. The achievement of the rule of law is to make personal influence unnecessary, so that a dentist in Denmark or the owner of a mid-sized manufacturer in Michigan keeps what they have without cultivating a minister. Money still flows into politics under such an order, but to get more, not to keep what one has; the distinction is the whole difference between lobbying and tribute. And taking the saying seriously makes things worse. The rich, fearing political vulnerability, buy influence to protect themselves, and so produce the oligarchic arrangement that makes everyone else regard their wealth as a threat. The prophecy funds its own fulfillment.

The saying also flatters the wealthy by understating what they already hold. Wealth is power before anyone buys a senator; it purchases time, lawyers and exit. The medieval financier facing expropriation and the modern billionaire facing a higher tax rate are not in the same situation, and the warning is usually deployed as though they were.

The distinction that matters is between wealth protected by institutions and wealth protected by proximity to rulers, and it is visible in what a rich person must do to sleep. Under constitutional government they can afford to be politically ordinary. Under personalist government they must join the regime, buy patrons or accept that ownership continues at someone's pleasure. The remedy is not to give every fortune a senator or a militia but to build an order in which none of that is required, because the moment wealth needs power to survive, rights have already stopped being enough, and the wealthy are merely the first to find out.
