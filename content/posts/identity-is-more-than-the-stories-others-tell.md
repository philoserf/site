title: Identity is more than the stories others tell
---

The claim that we aren't people, only "the stories others tell about us," reduces identity to its social residue. It gets one thing right and then claims too much.

At its most defensible, the claim describes how identity works in public. Other people assemble the version of you they carry from anecdotes, impressions, and secondhand accounts. When you die, that narrative residue is all that remains accessible to others. Historians don't encounter Caesar; they encounter texts about Caesar. In this sense, the social self is irreducibly storied. Paul Ricoeur argued that the stories we tell constitute personal identity itself, not just reputation—that the self is less a fixed entity than a coherent plot threading through time.

But the claim collapses a distinction by asserting we aren't people at all. It conflates the representation with the thing represented. The stories people tell about you are often wrong, self-serving, and incomplete, which makes sense only if there's something they fail to capture. A person correcting a false rumor about themselves appeals to what they did, remembered, and meant, not to a better story. The claim, taken literally, would make that correction incoherent.

What the claim touches is the gap between inner life and social existence: the strangeness of knowing yourself from the inside while others know you only through a thin stream of behavior. Most people feel, at some point, that others are reacting to a character rather than to them. The mistake is the move from "our social identity is largely narrative" to "that's all we are." No argument gets you there. It's a surrender, trading the complexity of a person for the cleaner, poorer ontology of a reputation.
