---
title: The mind as a parliament of conflicting parts
description: An hour after swearing off sugar, she faces a plate of cake. One faction cites the pact, another pleads a stressful day, a third picks up the fork. Tomorrow, which of them answers for it?
date: 2027-01-04
---

Put a plate of cake in front of someone who has just declared, out loud, with conviction, that she is done eating sugar, and watch the parliament convene. One faction cites the pact made an hour ago. Another produces evidence that today has been stressful and exceptions exist for stressful days. A third, saying nothing, simply picks up the fork. This is not weakness of will in the moral sense your grandmother meant. It is closer to a hostile committee meeting where the minutes were never agreed upon and the gavel belongs to whoever grabs it first.

Committees disagree; that's what they're for. The strange part is that all the delegates share one office, one signature, one body that will show up tomorrow to answer for what was decided tonight. You do not get to say "the fork-faction did it, not me." You wake up owning the whole transcript, including the parts you'd have voted against if anyone had bothered to poll you.

Freud gave this committee three named seats and imagined them locked in permanent negotiation, one urging appetite, one urging propriety, a third trying to keep the peace between them. Later psychologists traded his cast of characters for other metaphors—Haidt's elephant and rider, Kahneman's System 1 and System 2—but the diagnosis held steady under every relabeling: there is no single sovereign in there. There is a coalition government, and coalition governments are places where consensus is often manufactured after the fact rather than discovered in advance.

This is where the marvel turns to horror. Ask someone recovering from an affair, an addiction, an outburst they still don't fully understand, and you will hear the same bewildered refrain: _I don't know why I did that._ Not _I regret it_—regret implies you recognize the actor. This is stranger. It is looking at your own hand and finding someone else's fingerprints. The horror is discovering that "you," the entity you'd been assuming was a unified executive with final say over the body's actions, was in fact more like a press secretary—announcing decisions made upstream by processes that never told you.

And yet something in us keeps insisting on the fiction of unity, and for good reason. Courts do not sentence "the amygdala." No one proposes to "the limbic system." The felt experience of being one continuous person, promising things and later being held to those promises, is the load-bearing wall of every human relationship and every legal system built on a person who can be held responsible. So we live suspended between two true things: the psychological fact of multiplicity, and the practical, moral necessity of behaving as though we are singular. Neither truth is negotiable. You cannot fully believe you are one thing without lying about the data, and you cannot fully believe you are a committee without dissolving the very idea of a self who could be blamed, thanked, forgiven, or trusted.

There is comedy here too, next to the horror rather than against it. Consider the theatrical absurdity of watching yourself narrate your own betrayal in real time: setting three alarms because you know that the version of you who wakes at 6 a.m. will not honor the plans of the version who set them at midnight, like a man leaving increasingly desperate notes for a roommate who is also, somehow, himself. Or lying awake mentally rehearsing an argument with someone who wronged you, constructing devastating rebuttals for a conversation that isn't happening, because some subcommittee in the brain apparently believes rehearsal is the same as resolution. Anyone who has done this and then laughed at themselves doing it has, for a moment, achieved something rare: standing slightly outside the committee room, watching the delegates argue, without needing to pretend the argument isn't happening.

That vantage point—call it witnessing, call it the observer, call it whatever your contemplative tradition calls it—may be the closest thing to peace available to a divided mind. No one has eliminated the conflicting parts; this is only a seat with a slightly better view of the room. The alcoholic who says "a part of me wants a drink" instead of "I want a drink" has named the delegate instead of being occupied by it. Naming a faction doesn't expel it from the coalition, but it does downgrade it from sovereign to constituent, which turns out to be most of what negotiation ever accomplishes anywhere.

The mind remains a parliament that never adjourns, passing contradictory motions, occasionally surprising its own members with a 3 a.m. vote nobody remembers scheduling. To be human is to hold the pen and know, with some combination of amusement and dread, that you were not consulted on everything done in your name—and to show up anyway, tomorrow, to sign for it.
