The long argument over whether money is speech has been conducted almost entirely on the terrain of persuasion, and that is why it has produced so little. The Court says spending is expression because expression costs money. Critics say spending is power wearing expression's clothes. Both describe what happens when a message reaches a voter, and both describe the least consequential thing political money does.

The doctrine first. Buckley v. Valeo in 1976, extended by Citizens United in 2010, reasoned that political spending cannot be separated from political expression, because expression requires resources. You cannot publish without paying for paper or bandwidth. You cannot broadcast without buying time. Restrict the spending and you have restricted the speech. There is something right at the core of this. A statute capping book budgets at fifty dollars would be a speech restriction however carefully it was written to sound like an accounting rule.

The generalization is where it goes wrong. Once spending counts as speech, every independent expenditure aimed at political effect inherits First Amendment protection: corporate advertising, super PAC infrastructure, the unlimited outside campaign. The Court treated the dollar as a unit of vocabulary, so that limiting dollars became identical to limiting words.

The standard objection is that money is not speech but power, and it lands. Speech is propositional and answerable; it invites a reply and can be met with better speech. Power requires no such exchange. It secures access, deters challengers, determines which calls get returned. A donor writing ten million dollars and a voter writing ten are not doing the same thing, whatever the formal description says. One buys capacity, the other states a preference, and calling both speech wraps the First Amendment around the whole bundle.

But the objection stops where the doctrine stops, at the moment of persuasion. Speech and power are not opposites; a demagogue with no money can do enormous damage with a microphone. The interesting question was never how loudly a position gets argued.

It is which positions get argued at all. By the time a voter encounters a campaign message, the decisive work is done. Money determined which candidates were viable enough to attract staff and coverage, which policy options arrived in a legislator's office already drafted, which questions a committee scheduled, and which proposals everyone involved understood to be outside serious discussion before anyone took a position on them. Nobody was convinced of anything. The alternatives were not present to be evaluated, and their absence leaves no trace a voter could notice.

Political scientists have had a name for this since 1960, when E.E. Schattschneider wrote that the definition of the alternatives is the supreme instrument of power. The doctrine has none. Agenda-setting is not expressive in any sense the First Amendment recognizes, and not coercive in any way a campaign-finance statute can reach. It is a structural effect of many individually defensible transactions, which is why disclosure rules and contribution limits keep producing so little: they govern the visible stage of a process whose outcome was fixed offstage.

Democratic equality assumes political voice is distributed on different terms than wealth: one person, one vote, whatever the net worth. Money-as-speech creates a second franchise scaled to capital, and the second routinely sets the terms on which the first is exercised. The wealthy do not mainly speak louder. They decide what the conversation is about, early enough that the conversation feels like it arrived on its own.

Whether the Court got the doctrine wrong or merely incomplete is a smaller question than it looks. The elision that matters is not between money and speech. It is the assumption, shared by the justices and most of their critics, that political influence works by convincing people, when the most durable kind works by settling, in advance and out of view, what there is to be convinced about.
