---
title: "History of in-person constructed Magic: The Gathering"
description: In 2015, 7,551 players filled a Las Vegas hall for one Grand Prix. Five years later the Grand Prix was gone. Where did paper Magic's competitive life go, and what came back?
date: 2027-02-22
---

Constructed play is Magic played with a deck you built in advance from your own collection, as against Limited, where you build from packs opened on the spot. In person means paper cards across a table: the kitchen table, the store, the convention hall. Between the first house rules and the 2026 season, the way that play was organized was built, torn down, and rebuilt, and the place most of it happens moved from the tournament floor to the Commander table.

## Before formats (1993 to 1994)

Magic shipped in August 1993 with no deck-building rules beyond a forty-card minimum, and the first players built whatever the local card pool allowed. The first "tournaments" were store and convention pickup events run on whatever rules the organizer chose. Wizards formed the Duelists' Convocation, later the DCI, in late 1993 to sanction tournaments and write deck rules, and it was the DCI that settled the shape constructed play still has: a sixty-card minimum, no more than four of any card but basic lands, and a list of cards banned or restricted to one copy. Ante, the original rule that each player put up a card from the top of the deck as a stake, was dropped from sanctioned play at once.

The first World Championship was held at Gen Con in Milwaukee in August 1994 and won by Zak Dolan with a deck built from the whole card pool. Every sanctioned event was that one format, then called Type 1 and now Vintage.

## Formats and the Pro Tour (1995 to 1999)

In January 1995 the DCI split the game. Type 1 kept every card. Type 2, now Standard, allowed only the current core set and the last few expansions, and rotated as new sets arrived. The split let new players compete without owning the Power Nine (the nine most powerful early cards, Black Lotus among them) and gave Wizards a format tied to current product; it has been the center of constructed play since. Type 1.5, with the Type 1 restricted list as a banned list, served players between the two until it became Legacy in 2004.

The Pro Tour began in February 1996 in New York, invitation-only, with cash prizes and a schedule of about four events a year. Michael Loconto won the first. The Pro Tour made Magic a competitive career, produced its first stars, and gave constructed play a ladder: local Pro Tour Qualifiers (PTQs) fed each event, and the top finishers at one Pro Tour were invited to the next. National championships and the regional and state events below them filled out the year. The first Grand Prix, open to anyone who turned up, was held in Amsterdam in March 1997; Grand Prix became the mass-participation tier under the Pro Tour and ran until 2020.

Extended arrived in 1997 as a middle format, a few more years of sets than Standard with its own banned list, so that rotation did not strand a whole collection. Block Constructed, limited to one block, ran alongside it on the Pro Tour for a decade.

The late nineties also taught the game what constructed play could do to itself. Urza's Saga and its sequels produced "Combo Winter" of 1998 to 1999, decks that won on the second turn, and the DCI banned a run of cards within months. The Sixth Edition rules of 1999 rewrote the game's timing to make tournament play consistent, introducing the stack.

## The store becomes the center (2000 to 2010)

Friday Night Magic launched in 2000: a weekly, low-stakes sanctioned event in game stores, with promo cards for participation. It became the base of the whole structure, and the store, not the convention hall, became the place a player learned the current Standard.

The DCI ratings system, Elo-based, gave every sanctioned match a stake, and rating-based invites to the Pro Tour made some players stop playing to protect their numbers. It was replaced in 2011 by Planeswalker Points, which rewarded playing more.

In 2004 Type 1 was renamed Vintage and Type 1.5 became Legacy, with its own banned list; Legacy grew into a serious format over the following decade largely through independent organizers, above all StarCityGames, whose Open Series from 2009 became a national circuit outside Wizards' own ladder. The Pro Tour Hall of Fame opened in 2005.

The same years saw the quiet start of the format that would come to dominate casual constructed play. Elder Dragon Highlander, a multiplayer format with hundred-card singleton decks led by a legendary creature, was developed by judges in Alaska in the late 1990s and spread through the judge community; Sheldon Menery's rules committee ran it independently. Wizards released the first Commander decks in 2011 and named the format, which went on to outgrow every other way of playing paper Magic.

## Scale, then upheaval (2010 to 2019)

Modern arrived in 2011 and took over Extended's role, and Extended was retired in 2013. Modern is a non-rotating format starting at Eighth Edition, the first set in the modern card frame, with a banned list designed to keep turn-three kills out. It became the second pillar beside Standard and the main reason reprint sets exist. Pauper, commons only, became a sanctioned paper format in 2019. Pioneer, non-rotating from Return to Ravnica onward, was created in October 2019 as a cheaper Modern.

Grand Prix grew enormous. The Modern Masters Grand Prix in Las Vegas in 2013 drew about 4,500 players, and its 2015 sequel 7,551, the largest trading-card tournament ever held. Regional organizers such as ChannelFireball and StarCityGames ran dozens a year. Below them, PTQs were replaced in 2015 by store-level Preliminary PTQs feeding Regional PTQs, pushing qualification down into the stores.

Then the top tier changed shape. In 2019 the Pro Tour was renamed the Mythic Championship, Grand Prix became MagicFests, a salaried Magic Pro League and Rivals league took over the top of the game, and the Players Tour of 2020 was announced as the new regional second tier.

## Shutdown and rebuild (2020 to 2023)

The pandemic ended it. The last MagicFests ran in early March 2020, the Players Tour was canceled, stores closed, and for two years sanctioned paper play was close to nothing. Wizards used the gap to dissolve the leagues.

What came back in 2022 was a paper ladder again. Regional Championship Qualifiers (RCQs) in stores feed Regional Championships, run by regional organizers across the world, which feed a reinstated Pro Tour; the first of the new series, Pro Tour Phyrexia, was played in February 2023. Grand Prix did not return. In their place came MagicCons, a few large conventions a year that host the Pro Tour and side events, starting with MagicCon: Philadelphia in February 2023, home of Pro Tour Phyrexia. The World Championship now closes each season.

## Standard revived, Commander remade (2023 to 2026)

Wizards declared in 2023 that tabletop Standard needed reviving: rotation stretched from two years to three, the store-level RCQs were pointed at Standard, and the core set returned as Foundations, built to stay legal for five years. The 2025 and 2026 Standard sets included Universes Beyond crossovers for the first time, so Final Fantasy and Marvel cards sit in the game's main constructed format.

The Magic Spotlight Series started in 2025 as open, Grand Prix-like weekends tied to set releases with Pro Tour invites on offer; the 2026 schedule had twelve. The 2025 to 2026 season ended with the World Championship in November 2026, and the 2026 to 2027 season adds a one-off Limited Championship.

Commander changed hands. In September 2024 the Rules Committee banned four cards, among them Mana Crypt and Jeweled Lotus, and the harassment that followed drove the committee to dissolve itself on September 30, 2024, handing the format to Wizards. A Commander Format Panel under Gavin Verhey now runs it. Its main act was the bracket system of February 2025, five tiers from Exhibition to cEDH with a list of Game Changer cards to help a table agree on power level before the first game, revised in October 2025. For most people who play paper Magic in 2026, that table is where constructed play happens, and the bracket conversation has replaced the banned list as the rule that matters.
