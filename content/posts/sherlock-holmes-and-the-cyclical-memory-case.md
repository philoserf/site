---
title: Sherlock Holmes and the cyclical memory case
description: A fictional Sherlock Holmes tale in which Professor Moriarty's station-master brother discovers his meticulous train records reveal periodic memory lapses, leading Holmes to muse on memory's orbital, illusory nature.
date: 2026-12-16
---

It was in the latter part of the year '87 that a most singular case came to the attention of my friend, Mr. Sherlock Holmes. The affair began on a dreary November evening as we sat before the fire at 221B Baker Street, Holmes idly examining the agony column while I dozed over a medical journal.

A sharp rap broke the silence, and Mrs. Hudson entered to announce a visitor—a Mr. Moriarty.

"Not _the_ Moriarty? Not the professor?" I inquired, suddenly alert.

"No, Watson," Holmes replied, setting aside his paper. "The professor's elder brother, the station master at Waterloo. An altogether more respectable member of the family, though no less interesting for that."

Our visitor was a tall, stooping man with a high forehead and deep-set eyes that darted nervously about the room before settling on Holmes.

"Mr. Holmes," he began, "I find myself in the grip of a most disturbing situation. I fear I am losing my mind, or worse—that someone is systematically erasing it."

Holmes leaned forward, steepling his fingers beneath his chin. "Pray, take a seat and elaborate, Mr. Moriarty."

"I am a man of numbers, Mr. Holmes," Moriarty explained. "For thirty years, I have maintained meticulous records of every train that has entered or left Waterloo Station—arrivals, departures, delays, all precisely documented. I can recall with perfect clarity the scheduling peculiarities of any given day over the past three decades."

"A remarkable faculty," Holmes observed.

"So I believed, until last Tuesday." Moriarty withdrew a leather-bound journal from his coat. "I discovered discrepancies in my memory. When consulting my records of November 4th, 1885, I found entries in my own handwriting describing events I have no recollection of recording. More disturbing still, when I examined the station's official logs, they matched my written record, not my memory."

"Most curious," Holmes murmured. "And these discrepancies—do they form any pattern?"

"Indeed they do, which is why I sought you out. The forgotten events occur in a perfect cyclical pattern, as if a wheel were turning through my memory, systematically erasing certain moments while leaving others intact."

Holmes sprang from his chair. "Hand it here. Excellent! Watson, be so good as to fetch my commonplace books and calipers."

For the next hour, Holmes pored over Moriarty's journal, measuring the ink density of various entries, comparing handwriting samples, and constructing an elaborate table of the forgotten incidents.

"Look here, Watson," he said at last. "Our visitor's memory lapses occur at precise intervals—a mathematical sequence of remarkable elegance. The mean interval between forgotten events is exactly 27.3 days, with a probable error of merely 0.14. The distribution follows a perfect curve of error."

"But what does it mean, Holmes?" I asked.

"It means, my dear Watson, that this is no criminal case." Holmes turned to our visitor. "Mr. Moriarty, do you recall your brother's dissertation on the dynamics of asteroids?"

Moriarty started. "Why, yes—though few could follow it."

"Fewer than you think. Your brother posited that memory itself operates like a celestial body, subject to gravitational forces and orbital mechanics."

Holmes crossed to the window, gazing out at the fog-shrouded London streets. "What we are witnessing, gentlemen, is the orbital periodicity of human memory—a wheel of the mind, turning certain memories into the light and others into shadow."

"But surely," I protested, "a man knows what he remembers and what he does not!"

"That is precisely the illusion, Watson!" Holmes exclaimed. "We assume continuity of memory, yet the evidence before us suggests otherwise. Mr. Moriarty is unique only in having documented the phenomenon. The rest of humanity experiences the same memory cycles but lacks the data to perceive the pattern."

Holmes seized his pipe and began pacing the room. "We reach backward into memory, constructing narratives of our past that are largely fictional. We project forward, anticipating futures that rarely materialize as imagined. Meanwhile, the present moment—the only empirical reality we can truly observe—slips by unnoticed."

"You suggest this is… natural?" Moriarty asked, bewildered.

"Entirely natural, though no less extraordinary for that," Holmes replied. "The human mind, like any measuring instrument, has its limitations. Your meticulous record-keeping has allowed you to detect the discrepancy between objective events and subjective recollection."

That evening, after Moriarty had departed somewhat reassured, if not entirely convinced, I remarked to Holmes that the case seemed unusually philosophical for his tastes.

"On the contrary, Watson," he replied, tamping fresh tobacco into his pipe. "What is detection but the art of observing the present moment with absolute clarity? Most crimes go unsolved because observers are forever looking backward or forward, and miss the evidence before their eyes."

He struck a match and drew thoughtfully on his pipe. "The wheel turns, Watson, erasing what came before. Our only advantage lies in recognizing that fact and keeping our eyes on the present. It is the only true deduction any of us can make."
